\begin{abstract}
A reduced-order PDE solver should allow a practitioner to choose an
accuracy and computational cost after training. We present a nonlinear
manifold reduced-order model (NM-ROM) that provides this choice through
nested correction directions added to a frozen decoder. Correction rank
changes the trial space without retraining; solver tolerance and,
where applicable, empirical quadrature control the cost of the reduced
solve. The architecture combines a learned spatial bank with a small
nonlinear coefficient map, exact boundary enforcement, and matrix-free
weak residual projection. Linear operators are precomputed, while
validated quadrature reduces the cost of nonlinear residual evaluation.
Experiments on elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic problems compare the
resulting accuracy--cost tradeoff with neural operators, POD-based
reduced models and named full-order solvers. On Burgers, a fixed-test-space
correction ladder spans $\nQxmErrSpan\times$ in same-grid error for
$\nQxmCostSpan\times$ in runtime; a separate scheduled ladder is tested
across training seeds and a held-out cohort. Acceleration depends on the
comparator and resolution: selected reduced solves are faster than
iterative full-order methods, while efficient direct solvers and neural
operators remain stronger in several cases. Linear-bank controls further
identify when the nonlinear restriction adds cost without improving
accuracy. These results establish deployment-time tunability and delimit
its practical benefit, rather than a universal speed advantage.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Neural operators for partial differential equations (PDEs)
\citep{Li2020FNO,Lu2021DeepONet,Kovachki2023NeuralOperator,Li2024PINO,BoulleTownsend2024}
provide fast amortised inference. At a fixed grid and inference
procedure, a trained operator returns one accuracy and runtime point.
A practitioner may instead need to spend more computation on a difficult
query, or accept a less accurate solution when response time matters.
Classical full-order methods (FOMs) expose numerical controls such as
iteration tolerance, but their cost can grow substantially with mesh
resolution. The question is whether a trained reduced model can provide
a useful accuracy--cost tradeoff at deployment.

Projection-based reduced-order models address this question by solving
the governing equations in a smaller space
\citep{BennerGugercinWillcox2015,Sirovich1987POD}. A linear subspace is
inexpensive to use, but can require many basis functions to represent
moving fronts. Nonlinear-manifold ROMs learn a more compact
representation \citep{LeeCarlberg2020,KimChoi2022NMROM}; their online
nonlinear solve can, however, offset the benefit of fewer unknowns.
Representation accuracy and computational advantage therefore need to
be measured separately.

We present an NM-ROM with a deployment-time accuracy--cost family from
one trained decoder. The solution is represented by a learned spatial
bank multiplied by a nonlinear map of latent variables. We augment this
map with nested, precomputed correction directions. Activating more
directions enlarges the trial space without changing the learned
weights. The correction rank is the primary representation control;
iteration limits and stopping tolerances govern the numerical solve,
and empirical quadrature reduces residual-evaluation cost where its
validation permits. All online coefficients are determined from the
weak PDE residual, without access to the reference solution.

Our experiments ask whether this mechanism improves accuracy, what it
costs, and when it is competitive with established solvers. We retain
neural operators, POD models and the unrestricted learned bank as
comparators. Full-order timings name both the algorithm and its settings:
a speedup over iterative CG is distinct from a speedup over a direct
transform. The main evidence concerns the completed two-dimensional
experiments; the separately labelled three-dimensional development
panels extend the comparison without being treated as final tests.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A tunable NM-ROM from one trained model.}
Nested correction directions provide discrete deployment settings that
change approximation capacity without retraining. We isolate correction
rank at fixed residual test count and separately evaluate a prescribed
test-count schedule across seeds and held-out cases.
\item \textbf{An architecture supporting the accuracy--cost tradeoff.}
A learned spatial bank, a nonlinear head with a linear skip, and nested
corrections support compact latent solves, boundary enforcement and
node-local evaluation. Matched-dimension comparisons distinguish the
benefit of the nonlinear representation from that of the bank itself.
\item \textbf{A practical weak-form implementation and paired evaluation.}
Precomputed linear operators, matrix-free differentiation and validated
empirical quadrature make the online solve measurable against neural
operators and classical solvers. Paired accuracy and runtime measurements
identify the PDEs, settings and resolutions where acceleration occurs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Linear and non-linear manifold ROMs.}
Linear projection-based ROMs
\citep{BennerGugercinWillcox2015,Sirovich1987POD,Berkooz1993,QuarteroniManzoniNegri2015,HesthavenRozzaStamm2016}
suffer from the Kolmogorov $n$-width barrier on advection-dominated and
moving-front problems
\citep{CohenDeVore2015,GreifUrban2019,OhlbergerRave2016}. NM-ROMs lift this barrier with a non-linear trial manifold:
\citet{LeeCarlberg2020} introduced convolutional-autoencoder NM-ROMs with
LSPG projection \citep{Carlberg2011LSPG}, followed by shallow masked
autoencoders with sample-mesh hyper-reduction \citep{KimChoi2022NMROM},
reduced over-collocation \citep{RomorStabileRozza2023}, domain-decomposed
sparse autoencoders \citep{Diaz2024DomainDecompNMROM}, quadratic
manifolds \citep{GeelenWrightWillcox2022,BarnettFarhat2022},
feature-tracking variants with EQ \citep{MirhoseiniZahr2023},
POD-pre-compressed DL-ROMs \citep{FrescaManzoni2022PODDLROM} and
neural-field ROMs \citep{KimWenLeeChoiCNFROM2024}. We differ from these by a separable decoder --- a coordinate-network
bank \citep{Tancik2020FourierFeatures} times a small head with a linear
skip --- augmented by nested \emph{correction} directions chosen offline
once, so that a rank $q$ is selected at run time without refit, a family
from one trained decoder analogous to runtime-tunable networks
\citep{YuSlimmable2019,Cai2020OnceForAll}. NN-augmented projection ROMs
\citep{BarnettFarhatMaday2023} and online-adaptive bases
\citep{PeherstorferWillcox2015Adaptive,Carlberg2015hRefinement} add a
nonlinear correction to a linear basis; ours is the reverse composition,
the nonlinear head inside the linear bank with the linear part as the
correction. The projection is least-squares Petrov--Galerkin
\citep{Carlberg2011LSPG} and the linear terms are preassembled as in
tensorial POD \citep{StefanescuSandu2014,WederSchwerdtnerPeherstorfer2024};
we claim no novelty for either.

\paragraph{Neural operators, hyper-reduction, and differentiable programming.}
Neural-operator surrogates
\citep{Li2020FNO,Lu2021DeepONet,Kovachki2023NeuralOperator,LiGeoFNO2023,TranFFNO2023,Cao2021GalerkinTransformer,LiOFormer2023,HaoGNOT2023,WuTransolver2024,WangLNO2024,WenGAOT2025,HuLinearNO2025,Shikhman2026BoundaryIndexed}
and PDE foundation models
\citep{HerdePoseidon2024,McCabeMPP2024,HaoDPOT2024,HolzschuhPDETransformer2025}
supply a fixed operating point when grid and inference procedure are held
fixed, and typically approximate boundary conditions via penalty terms, with
partial fixes via distance functions or hard constraints
\citep{SukumarSrivastava2022,BerroneBC2022,BrechtHardConstraints2023,WangBENO2024,LippePDERefiner2023};
hybrid solver-in-the-loop methods
\citep{UmSolverInTheLoop2020,Kochkov2021PNAS,KopanicakovaKarniadakis2024,EshaghiNOWS2025,Raissi2019,Karniadakis2021PIML}
embed operators in classical iterations. We instead use the network solely for manifold learning with a
least-squares Petrov--Galerkin projection \citep{Carlberg2011LSPG} for
the residual, and compare against an FNO, a U-Net
\citep{Ronneberger2015UNet,Takamoto2022PDEBench} and a Transolver
\citep{WuTransolver2024} on identical data at an equal wall budget
(\S\ref{sec:results:operators}). For hyper-reduction we retain classical EQ fitted by non-negative least squares (NNLS)
\citep{Hernandez2017EQ,YanoPatera2019LPEQ,LawsonHanson1974NNLS} over DEIM
\citep{ChaturantabutSorensen2010}, ECSW
\citep{FarhatECSW2014,Chapman2017AcceleratedECSW}, GNAT
\citep{Carlberg2013GNAT} and their neural and high-order variants
\citep{NegriManzoniAmsallem2015,HirschPichiHesthavenNEIM2024,Nguyen2024HighOrderEIM};
the difference is that only the nonlinear term is sampled and that a
fitted rule is accepted by its held-out error and by re-draws, not by
its fit residual. On separable cells the discrete sine transform \citep{Swarztrauber1977}
is a direct solve faster than every reduced model we measure;
preconditioned Krylov methods \citep{HestenesStiefel1952CG,Saad2003} are
the iterative comparator.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

We turn a learned manifold into a practical PDE solver through three
components: a trial manifold whose decoder enforces Dirichlet conditions
exactly and carries nested linear correction directions
(\S\ref{sec:method:manifold}); a least-squares Petrov--Galerkin
projection of the discrete residual onto fixed weak tests
(\S\ref{sec:method:weak}); and exact preassembly of every linear term
with Empirical Quadrature for Burgers advection where validated
(\S\ref{sec:method:precomp}); \S\ref{sec:decoder} gives the architecture.
Throughout, $u \in \Real^{n}$ is the
full-order state on a uniform grid of $N$ intervals per axis,
$\Aop \in \Real^{n \times n}$ the negative five-point Laplacian on
$[0,1]^2$; $R$ is the bank width, $k$ the latent dimension, $M$ the number
of weak tests and $m$ the number of quadrature nodes.
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} (Appendix~\ref{app:arch}) shows the data flow;
Appendix~\ref{app:method} gives the per-PDE derivations and exit codes.

\subsection{Trial Manifold with Exact Dirichlet Enforcement}
\label{sec:method:manifold}

We train a decoder $\mathcal{D} : \Real^k \to \Real^n$, $k \ll n$, with
latent state $z$ and no encoder in the deployed path, separable into a
frozen spatial \emph{bank} $\bank\in\Real^{n\times R}$ times a small
neural \emph{head} $\head:\Real^{k}\to\Real^{R}$, $k\le R\ll n$, the bank
built once per mesh from a random-Fourier-feature coordinate network
$g_\phi$ \citep{Tancik2020FourierFeatures},
\begin{equation}
  \bank_{x,:} = \bcmask(x)\,g_\phi(x)\T,
  \qquad
  \bcmask(x) = 16\,x_1(1-x_1)\,x_2(1-x_2).
  \label{eq:bank}
\end{equation}
To strictly enforce the homogeneous Dirichlet condition on the boundary
$\Gamma$, we utilize the smooth vanishing factor $\bcmask$, zero on
$\Gamma$, folded into every column of the bank, so that
$\partial u/\partial z = 0$ on $\Gamma$ at every resolution (homogeneous
data only).

\paragraph{Nested correction directions.}
\label{sec:method:ladder}
The deployed model augments the head's output with $q$ fixed directions
$\corr\in\Real^{R\times q}$ and solves for the latent code and the
correction coefficients together,
\begin{equation}
  u(\latent,y) \;=\; \bank\big(\head(\latent) + \corr\,y\big),
  \qquad \latent\in\Real^{k},\; y\in\Real^{q},\; 0\le q\le R .
  \label{eq:ladder}
\end{equation}
The columns of $\corr$ are chosen offline from training data; the
Burgers construction uses principal components of the head's coefficient
errors. Per-PDE constructions are stated in Appendix~\ref{app:method}.
The directions are ordered so that $C_{q}$ is a prefix of $C_{q'}$ for $q<q'$; the
ladder is nested and no rung needs retraining. At $q=0$ the model is the
frozen head; at $q=R$ the head is irrelevant and the representation is the full
linear span of the bank. For linear PDEs its coefficients can be found
by a reduced linear solve; for nonlinear PDEs the equations can remain
nonlinear even in this full-bank representation;
in between, $q$ moves the reachable set from the head's image towards
the bank's span, and the solved dimension from $k$ to $k+q$. Two consequences follow: the bank's projection error is a floor on the
achievable error whatever the head or the solver does, so nonlinearity
in $\head$ buys a smaller \emph{solved} dimension, not an escape from the
span; and all $x$-dependence factors through $\bank$, which makes node
sampling and exact preassembly available from one decoder.

\subsection{Projecting the PDE onto the Manifold}
\label{sec:method:weak}

Each PDE we consider gives a discretised residual $\rfull(u)$ that
should vanish at the true solution. We substitute the trial manifold
$u(\latent,y)$ into $\rfull$ and project onto a fixed, latent-independent
test space: $\tests\in\Real^{M\times n}$ collects the $M$ lowest
tensor-product sine vectors of the square, eigenvectors of the five-point
operator, $\tests\Aop=\rowscale\tests$. The reduced problem solved online
is
\begin{equation}
  (\latent^{\star},y^{\star})
  =
  \argmin_{\latent,\,y}\;
  \tfrac12\,\norm{\rweak(\latent,y)}_2^2,
  \qquad
  \rweak(\latent,y) = \rowscale_\star^{-1}\,\tests\,\rfull\!\big(u(\latent,y)\big)
  \in\Real^{M},
  \qquad M>k+q ,
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
with a diagonal row scaling $\rowscale_\star$ stated per PDE in
Appendix~\ref{app:method}: a least-squares Petrov--Galerkin condition
with an explicit test space \citep{Carlberg2011LSPG,LeeCarlberg2020}, not
tangent Galerkin, overdetermined rather than square, solved by a method
appropriate to each PDE's structure.

\paragraph{Elliptic (Poisson).}
The Poisson residual is $\rfull(u) = \Aop u - f$, and projection gives
$\rweak(\latent,y)=B_0(\head(\latent)+\corr y)-b_0$, $B_0=\tests\bank$
(Appendix~\ref{app:method:poisson}); the projector onto
$\operatorname{range}(B_0\corr)$ does not depend on $\latent$, so $y$ is
eliminated exactly \citep{GolubPereyra1973}, the iteration stays
$k$-dimensional at every $q$, and $q=R$ is a linear solve.

\paragraph{Parabolic (heat).}
The heat semi-discretisation $du/dt = -\kappa\, \Aop u$ is advanced with
Crank--Nicolson; the reduced step substitutes the manifold into the fully
discrete equation \emph{before} projecting and solves
$\latent_{n+1}=\argmin_{\latent}\norm{B_0\head(\latent)-D\,B_0\head(\latent_n)}_2$
(Appendix~\ref{app:method:heat}), a nonlinear least-squares problem, not
a linear system; at $q=R$ the trajectory is the exact modal propagation
of the bank coefficients.

\paragraph{Hyperbolic (Burgers).}
For $u_t+u(u_x+u_y)=\nu\Delta u$ with a sign-upwind stencil and backward
Euler (Appendix~\ref{app:method:burgers}) the weak residual is nonlinear
in the coefficients, so $y$ is not eliminated in closed form; we damp the
$(\latent,y)$ blocks separately inside one Levenberg--Marquardt step
(\emph{block-damped} variable projection), which, in the recorded solver comparison, removes the joint LM
configuration's $\nBlockJointBudgetExits$ budget exits
(Table~\ref{tab:solver-variants}).

\paragraph{Solver and exits.}
Each attempt solves the damped normal system
$(H+\lambda\,\diag(\diag H))\,\delta=-g$, $H=\jac{}\T\jac{}$,
$g=\jac{}\T\rweak$, accepting the step only if it strictly decreases the
residual: damped Levenberg--Marquardt with a monotone test
\citep{Marquardt1963}. Three exit families are recorded and never conflated: the scale-free
stationarity measure
$\station{\latent}{y}=\norm{\jac{}\T\rweak}_2/(\normF{\jac{}}\norm{\rweak}_2)\le\eta_{\mathrm{tol}}$
\refstepcounter{equation}\label{eq:stationarity}(\theequation), a
residual threshold, and the stalls, reported as early-stopped, never as
converged. No query uses the solution it predicts: the elliptic solve starts from
the cached training code nearest the projected source, the Burgers query
fits $(\latent,y)$ to the supplied initial field
(Appendix~\ref{app:method:codes}).

\subsection{Hyper-reduction}
\label{sec:method:precomp}

The non-linear manifold reduces the DOF count from $n$ to $k+q$, but a
projected term such as $\tests N(\bank c)$ still costs $O(n)$. For a fixed linear operator
the tested residual is exactly precomputable,
$\tests(\Aop\,u-f)=B\,(\head(\latent)+\corr y)-b$ with
$B=\rowscale\,\tests\bank$ assembled offline by two sine transforms, so
linear terms are never approximated; the Burgers advection
$\tests N(\bank c)$ is the only term that resists preassembly, evaluated
\emph{densely}, $O(nR)$ per residual, or on an \emph{empirical quadrature}
rule of $m$ nodes \citep{Hernandez2017EQ,YanoPatera2019LPEQ}, $O(mR)$. The rule is a non-negative \emph{per-node} weight vector, \emph{independent
of the snapshot}, fitted by NNLS \citep{LawsonHanson1974NNLS} to reproduce
the projected advection term at $n_{\rm fit}$ stored codes with a hard cap
of $m$ nodes (Appendix~\ref{app:method:eq}); changing $m$ re-solves the
fit, rules are not nested, and deployment selects among stored rules. \emph{A rule is never accepted on its NNLS fit residual}; we score it by
the held-out relative error $\rho$ of the projected advection term
\eqref{eq:rho} over states the solver actually reaches on trajectories
disjoint from the fit and evaluation cases, against a primary bar
$\rho_{\max}\le\nEqtopBar$ fixed before any certification job ran and a
tight bar $\nEqtopTightBar$, and then re-draw the construction: a rule is
\emph{confirmed} only if every re-draw passes
(\S\ref{sec:results:tunability}).

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:decoder}

Three properties of the problem motivate our architecture: the latent
iteration is initialised cold, so the decoder Jacobian must retain a
well-conditioned linear component, which motivates a \emph{linear skip}
in the head; hyper-reduction retains a sparse subset of mesh nodes, so
the decoder must evaluate at any node in mesh-independent time, which
motivates a \emph{per-node bank}; and the family must reach from the
head's image to the bank's whole span without retraining, which
motivates the nested \emph{correction directions}.

\paragraph{Linear skip, for cold-start convergence.}
The head is a two-layer SiLU MLP plus a linear skip,
$\head(\latent)=\varphi_\theta(\latent)+W\T\latent$, so its Jacobian
retains a latent-independent component along which the cold solve can
descend; the elliptic solver checks the numerical rank of the projected
Jacobian at every query. The skip is a design choice, not ablated here.

\paragraph{No encoder; per-node bank.} No encoder is needed: the query
fits $(\latent,y)$ against the supplied input. The decoder restricted to
a rule's support is a cached block times the coefficients, and the
network's parameters do not depend on the mesh.

\paragraph{What is fixed, what is chosen, and how error is reported.}
\label{sec:method:tuning}
Every operating point uses one frozen artefact per PDE; selected at run
time are the rank $q$, the quadrature (dense or a stored rule) and the
stopping tolerance and budget; $M$ is held fixed along the headline ladder.
\label{sec:method:layers}
The representation ablations distinguish three quantities: the \emph{bank floor} (projection
error onto $\operatorname{range}\bank$), the \emph{best-found} error (the
smallest any point of the augmented manifold attains, from a multistart
oracle) and the \emph{solved} error the iteration returns.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

\subsection{Matrix-free Projected Operators via JAX}
Every linear term is preassembled once per mesh as the $M\times R$
matrix $B$, so the online residual and Jacobian of a linear PDE are
$B\,(\head(\latent)+\corr y)-b$ and $B\,[\dhead\;\;\corr]$, with $\dhead$
from a forward-mode Jacobian-Vector Product \citep{Bradbury2018JAX}; the
sampled Burgers advection uses the cached stencil block. The damped
normal system is solved directly; no Krylov solve is applied to the
projected operator.

\subsection{Training Protocol}

The bank and the head are trained in two stages, both as auto-decoders:
the coordinate network $g_\phi$ is fitted to the training states through
\eqref{eq:bank}, then frozen, and the head is fitted with a code library
$Z$ on the bank-projected states; $\corr$ is then constructed from training-only coefficient directions
as specified for each PDE. Sizes, cohorts
and offline cost are in Appendix~\ref{app:training-schedule}; every
headline uses one checkpoint per PDE, named by hash in
Table~\ref{tab:provenance}.

\section{Experimental Setup and Benchmark Problems}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Problems and references.}
The completed two-dimensional study includes viscous Burgers, Poisson,
heat, reflective waves and incompressible Navier--Stokes. Poisson is
also tested on an L-shaped domain, where a sine-transform solver does
not apply. Table~\ref{tab:problems} gives meshes and cohorts;
Table~\ref{tab:spec} specifies the sampled families. Burgers provides
the principal correction-rank study. Poisson, heat and waves test the
benefit of nonlinear restriction for linear equations. Navier--Stokes
and lower-viscosity Burgers test its limitations. The three-dimensional
development panels are reported separately in
Appendix~\ref{app:campaign-development} with their own references and
training contracts.

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
We report worst relative $L^2$ error over the stated cases and requested
times, in percent. Poisson uses the steady reference norm; heat and
wave displacement use the current reference norm. Burgers uses the
initial-field norm and distinguishes all-times error, including input
compression, from evolved-times error. Wave displacement alone does not
establish velocity or energy accuracy. Same-grid error measures reduction
error relative to the converged discrete solution. Error against a
refined reference also includes discretisation error; the two are never
interchanged. Development results and held-out tests are labelled
separately. Settings for a held-out test are fixed before its cases are
accessed.

\paragraph{Timing and speedup.}
Every accuracy--runtime pair comes from the same solver invocation.
We use double precision, GPU burn-in, synchronised timing and medians
of retained repetitions. GPU query time includes initialization, the
solve or rollout, and requested device outputs. Complete-query time
additionally includes host transfers and is labelled separately.
Training and compilation are offline costs. Speedup is
$S=T_{\mathrm{FOM}}/T_{\mathrm{method}}$, using measurements from the same
allocation. Each table identifies the FOM algorithm, tolerance and error.
Where a table selects the fastest tested passing FOM with no greater
error than a method, that selection and the possibly differing
denominators are explicit. A speedup is not an accuracy-equivalence
claim. Nonconverged solves and failed accuracy targets remain visible;
only settings satisfying the stated numerical checks enter a frontier.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare with FNO, U-Net, DeepONet and Transolver where matched runs
are available; missing measurements are not inferred from another
cohort. POD models use the recorded reduced formulation and stopping
rules. The \emph{linear ROM using the learned bank} bypasses the
nonlinear head and solves directly for bank coefficients; its speedups
are not attributed to nonlinear NM-ROM. Full-order controls include
iterative CG for applicable linear systems, Newton--Krylov for Burgers,
and the recorded time integrator for Navier--Stokes. Direct transforms
and sparse direct solvers remain explicit alternatives. Solver settings,
training budgets, failures and timing outliers are retained in the
appendix and machine-readable evidence.

\subsection{Which Knob to Turn}
\label{sec:results:tunability}

The primary control is correction rank $q$. Increasing it enlarges the
trial space without retraining, but does not guarantee monotone error
for a nonlinear solver. The fixed-test-space Burgers study measures
$\nQxmErrSpan\times$ lower error for $\nQxmCostSpan\times$ greater
runtime. A smaller fixed test space gives only
$\nQxmTwoFiftySixErrSpan\times$ error improvement
(Table~\ref{tab:qxm}), demonstrating why rank and residual resolution
must be separated.

\paragraph{Numerical controls and quadrature.}\label{sec:exp:eq}
Tolerance and iteration limits control numerical work; they cannot
remove a representation error floor. Quadrature approximates residual
evaluation and is distinct from adding correction directions. The
Burgers study compares sampled residuals with dense counterparts and
validates rules on states reached by the solver. A small training fit
residual is insufficient. Repeated construction confirms the rules at
$q=\nEqtopConfirmedRungs$; higher-rank results carry their single-draw
or marginal status (Appendix~\ref{app:extended:eq}). Heat, Poisson and
waves use precomputed linear operators rather than an online EQ knob.
The current three-dimensional development implementations do not
establish an EQ acceleration claim.

\subsection{The Head against Linear Maps at Matched Dimension}
\label{sec:results:head}

The learned bank and the nonlinear head answer different questions.
At matched latent dimension on Burgers, the head reaches
$\nAblBurgersNeural\,\%$ against $\nAblBurgersLinear\,\%$ for the
fitted affine map and $\nAblBurgersPodSixteen\,\%$ for POD-16
(Table~\ref{tab:head-burgers}). This is a representation advantage at
matched dimension, not a runtime advantage. On Poisson, the nonlinear
head can likewise outperform a matched-dimensional affine map while
losing in runtime to a larger POD model
(Table~\ref{tab:head-poisson}). Projection diagnostics and online solves
are kept separate: a best-found fit to a known reference is not a
prediction available at deployment.

\subsection{Linear PDEs, Navier--Stokes, and the L-shaped Domain}
\label{sec:results:collapse}

For linear equations, solving directly in the learned bank removes the
nonlinear iteration. This baseline can be both more accurate and faster
than NM-ROM, as the CG comparison illustrates. Its benefit belongs to
the reduced linear formulation; the nonlinear restriction is useful
only if its representational economy compensates for the online solve.
The POD comparisons are essential because the learned bank itself need
not be the best linear basis (Table~\ref{tab:linear}).

The two-dimensional Navier--Stokes study does not establish the planned
held-out advantage over matched-dimensional POD. Its exploratory
correction ladder therefore remains a limitation test, with all failed
criteria reported (Tables~\ref{tab:ns} and \ref{tab:ns-ladder}).
Lower-viscosity Burgers produces a stronger reduced-versus-reduced
comparison, but its mesh is under-resolved and it does not establish
superiority over the full-order solver
(Appendix~\ref{app:extended:lowvisc}). The separate three-dimensional
development panels include all four PDEs and operator families; they
are labelled by cohort and are not promoted to final generalisation
claims while the frozen evaluations are being completed.

On L-shaped Poisson, where the direct transform is unavailable, the
corrected NM-ROM is faster than the cheapest tested same-job FOM at
larger error. POD is cheaper still, with somewhat greater error than
the corrected head. Table~\ref{tab:lshape-main} reports both accuracy and
complete-query time, showing the practical tradeoff without attributing
the linear baseline's advantage to the nonlinear model.

\paragraph{Limitations.}\label{sec:limitations}
The fixed-test-space tuning result uses one checkpoint; the separately
scheduled ladder has multiple seeds and a held-out test. Nonlinear
initialization can converge to an inaccurate branch. Cohorts are small,
operator training is budget-limited, and some quadrature constructions
remain sensitive to the draw. Same-grid improvement can be limited by
discretisation error against a refined reference. The completed main
study uses structured two-dimensional discretisations; the appended
three-dimensional evidence is developmental. We provide no general
convergence guarantee, universal speedup claim or cold-start comparison
with every existing NM-ROM architecture.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a tunable NM-ROM whose nested corrections change
approximation capacity after training. The learned bank and nonlinear
head support a compact weak-form solve, while precomputation and
validated quadrature control its evaluation cost. The experiments
separate correction-rank accuracy gains from numerical stopping effects
and measure runtime against explicitly named baselines. Burgers supplies
the clearest tuning evidence; linear PDEs show when a direct reduced
solve is preferable, and the remaining comparisons delimit the current
method's accuracy and cost. Future work concerns more reliable nonlinear
initialization, resolved advection-dominated tests and extension beyond
structured meshes.
