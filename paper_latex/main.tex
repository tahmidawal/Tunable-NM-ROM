\begin{abstract}
Neural operators such as Fourier Neural Operators and DeepONets deliver
one (accuracy, speed) operating point per trained model; moving it
requires retraining. We present a nonlinear-manifold reduced-order model
(NM-ROM) that exposes a deployment-time accuracy/speed tradeoff from a
single trained model. After training, its spatial bank is ordered once by
importance, so a query chooses how many bank functions to use, and
whether to solve with a nonlinear head or in the linear span of the bank,
without changing a learned weight. The framework combines a
coordinate-network bank with exact Dirichlet enforcement, least-squares
Petrov--Galerkin projection of the discrete residual onto fixed tests
with every linear term precomputed, matrix-free JAX evaluation, and
empirical quadrature for the nonlinear term, confirmed on held-out states
the solver reaches. On viscous Burgers at $4096^2$ it reaches
$\nBurgDevAccErrFortyNinetySix\,\%$ error at
$\nBurgDevAccSFortyNinetySix\times$ the speed of Newton--BiCGStab, or
1.9\,\% at 21.7$\times$. At a fixed error, its query time on Burgers
barely grows with the mesh (under $2\times$ from $256^2$ to $4096^2$),
while the solver's grows about $30\times$. At matched latent dimension it
is more accurate than a reproduced NM-ROM and POD-LSPG. On three-dimensional Navier--Stokes at $96^3$ the nonlinear head
reaches $\nNsHeadErr\,\%$ at $\nNsHeadS\times$ the speed of the
pseudo-spectral solver that generated the data, and on three-dimensional
Burgers at $256^3$ the span reaches 2.2\,\% at 2.2$\times$ (3.8\,\% at
5.6$\times$) on sealed held-out cases. On linear Poisson and heat
problems in two and three dimensions the ordered span is the best
setting, reaching $\nBwPoisAccErr\,\%$ at $\nBwPoisAccS\times$ the speed of
conjugate gradients on Poisson at $4096^2$ and $\nHeatWideAccErr\,\%$ at
$\nHeatWideBatchedAccS\times$ that of Crank--Nicolson CG on held-out heat
cases at $2048^2$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Two properties decide whether a learned model can stand in for a
partial differential equation (PDE) solver: whether accuracy can be
traded for speed when the model is used, and how fast the cost of a
given accuracy grows as the mesh is refined. Full-order methods (FOMs)
solved with iterative solvers such as conjugate gradients
\citep{HestenesStiefel1952CG} have the first property, since their
tolerance and time step can be set per query, but their cost grows
quickly with the mesh. Neural operators
\citep{Li2020FNO,Lu2021DeepONet,Kovachki2023NeuralOperator} are fast to
evaluate, but at a given mesh each trained model gives one (accuracy,
wall-clock) point, and moving it requires retraining. Reduced-order
models (ROMs) sit in between: linear projection-based ROMs
\citep{BennerGugercinWillcox2015,Sirovich1987POD} can require many basis
functions for moving fronts \citep{CohenDeVore2015}, and
nonlinear-manifold ROMs (NM-ROMs) learn a more compact representation
\citep{LeeCarlberg2020,KimChoi2022NMROM}, but their online nonlinear
solve can offset the benefit of fewer unknowns. We ask: \emph{can one
trained model have both properties, an accuracy choice at query time and
a cost that grows slowly with the mesh?}

We answer this question for viscous Burgers in two dimensions and
incompressible Navier--Stokes in three, and use the same framework on
linear Poisson and heat problems. Our NM-ROM represents the solution by a
learned spatial bank multiplied by the output of a small nonlinear head.
After training, the bank is ordered once by how much of the training data
each of its functions carries. A query then chooses how many of the
ordered functions to use, $R'$, and whether to solve with the nonlinear
head or in the linear span of those functions, without changing a learned
weight. All online coefficients come from the weak PDE residual, without
access to the reference solution, and stopping tolerances and empirical
quadrature set the cost of the solve.

On viscous Burgers one frozen model covers
$\nBurgDevAccErrFortyNinetySix\,\%$ error at
$\nBurgDevAccSFortyNinetySix\times$ the speed of Newton--BiCGStab and
1.9\,\% at 21.7$\times$ at $4096^2$ (Table~\ref{tab:headline}). From
$1024^2$ one of its settings is more accurate and faster than the FNO,
U-Net, Transolver and DeepONet we trained at each mesh
(Table~\ref{tab:operators}; on Burgers these saw fewer training
trajectories than our model, \S\ref{sec:results:neuralops}), and at matched latent
dimension it is more accurate than a reproduced NM-ROM and POD-LSPG
(Table~\ref{tab:nmrom-baselines}). On three-dimensional Navier--Stokes the
nonlinear head is the accurate setting, $\nNsHeadErr\,\%$ at
$\nNsHeadS\times$ the speed of the pseudo-spectral solver at $96^3$, and at
$64^3$ and $96^3$ it is more accurate and faster than all four operators. On the
linear Poisson and heat problems the ordered span is the best setting, up
to $\nBwPoisAccS\times$ faster than conjugate gradients on Poisson and
$\nHeatWideBatchedAccS\times$ faster than Crank--Nicolson CG on held-out
heat cases.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Deployment-time tunability from a single trained NM-ROM.}
An importance-ordered bank gives nested settings of its width $R'$, and
the choice between the nonlinear head and the linear span sets the
accurate end. Accuracy and cost are chosen at query time without
retraining, and we measure the full ladders on development and held-out
cases.
\item \textbf{Accuracy and speed on nonlinear PDEs at large meshes.} On
Burgers up to $4096^2$ and on three-dimensional Navier--Stokes, the same
frozen model is faster than the named full-order solver at the finest
meshes, and it is more accurate and faster than the neural operators
we trained there and more accurate than prior NM-ROMs. Its empirical quadrature is confirmed on held-out states that the
solver reaches.
\item \textbf{An architecture and implementation that make this
practical.} A coordinate-network bank with exact Dirichlet enforcement, a
head with a linear skip, precomputed linear terms and matrix-free JAX
evaluation keep the query cost nearly flat as the mesh is refined. At a
fixed error our query time grows as about $N^{0.1}$ on Burgers and
$N^{0.4}$ on Navier--Stokes, against $N^{0.65}$ and $N^{1.0}$ for the
full-order solvers (Figure~\ref{fig:cost-scaling}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Linear and non-linear manifold ROMs.}
Linear ROMs approximate solutions in a fixed subspace
\citep{BennerGugercinWillcox2015,Sirovich1987POD}; moving fronts can
require a large basis \citep{CohenDeVore2015,GreifUrban2019}.
Nonlinear-manifold ROMs combine learned representations with residual
projection \citep{LeeCarlberg2020,KimChoi2022NMROM}, including quadratic
manifolds \citep{GeelenWrightWillcox2022,BarnettFarhat2022}, neural-field
representations \citep{KimWenLeeChoiCNFROM2024} and adaptive bases
\citep{PeherstorferWillcox2015Adaptive,Carlberg2015hRefinement}.
Least-squares Petrov--Galerkin projection
\citep{Carlberg2011LSPG} and preassembly of reduced operators
\citep{StefanescuSandu2014,WederSchwerdtnerPeherstorfer2024} are established
components. We differ from these by a coordinate-network bank with an
exact boundary factor, a head with a linear skip, and a bank ordered
after training whose nested widths give deployment-time settings from one
trained model, as runtime-tunable networks do
\citep{YuSlimmable2019,Cai2020OnceForAll}.

\paragraph{Neural operators and hyper-reduction.}
FNO \citep{Li2020FNO}, DeepONet \citep{Lu2021DeepONet}, U-Net
\citep{Ronneberger2015UNet,Takamoto2022PDEBench} and Transolver
\citep{WuTransolver2024} learn solution maps. Their evaluation resolution
or inference procedure can also change accuracy and cost; our mechanism
instead changes the trial space used to solve the PDE. Empirical
quadrature \citep{Hernandez2017EQ,YanoPatera2019LPEQ} reduces nonlinear
residual evaluation through non-negative weights. We retain that
construction, testing its error on states reached by the solver and
through independent re-draws. The main iterative linear-system
comparator is conjugate gradient \citep{HestenesStiefel1952CG}.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

To answer whether a single trained model can expose a deployment-time
accuracy/speed tradeoff, we turn a learned manifold into a PDE solver
through three components. First, we define a trial manifold whose decoder
enforces Dirichlet conditions exactly and whose spatial bank is ordered, so
that a query can use fewer of its functions (\S\ref{sec:method:manifold}).
Second, we project the full-order residual onto that manifold against
fixed test vectors, which reduces every query to a small least-squares
problem (\S\ref{sec:method:weak}). Third, we compute every linear term
once, offline, and apply Empirical Quadrature to the nonlinear term, so
that the cost of a query does not depend on the mesh size
(\S\ref{sec:method:precomp}). The architecture in \S\ref{sec:decoder} is
then designed to support these requirements in practice. Throughout,
$u\in\Real^{N}$ denotes the full-order discrete state on a uniform grid and
$K\in\Real^{N\times N}$ the sparse finite-difference Laplacian.

\subsection{Trial Manifold with Exact Dirichlet Enforcement}
\label{sec:method:manifold}

We do not use an encoder. The decoder writes the state as a fixed set of
spatial functions times coefficients produced by a small network,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde u(z) = \mathcal D(z) = G\,h(z),
  \qquad
  G_{x,:} = \mu(x)\,g_\phi(x)\T,
  \qquad
  \mu(x) = 16\,x_1(1-x_1)\,x_2(1-x_2).
  \label{eq:bank}
\end{equation}
The bank $G\in\Real^{N\times R}$ holds $R$ spatial functions, one per
column, each a coordinate network $g_\phi$ read at the grid nodes, and the
head $h:\Real^{k}\to\Real^{R}$ maps the latent state $z$ to their
coefficients. Since $\mu=0$ on the boundary $\Gamma$, every column of $G$
vanishes there, so $\partial\tilde u/\partial z=0$ on $\Gamma$ at any
resolution and the boundary equations drop out of the projection. The cube
and the L-shaped domain use a factor that vanishes on their boundaries in
the same way. A query finds $z$ by solving the PDE
(\S\ref{sec:method:weak}), starting from the nearest stored training code.

\paragraph{Ordering the bank.}
\label{sec:method:ladder}\label{sec:method:ordered}
Nothing in training orders the columns of $G$, so its first $R'$ columns
are a poor basis. After training we rotate it once,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat G = G\,T
  \label{eq:ordered}
\end{equation}
(Appendix~\ref{app:method:ordered}). The columns of $\widehat G$ are
orthonormal and ordered by how much of the training data they carry, so
the first $R'$ of them are the best $R'$-dimensional subspace of the bank
for the training fields. A query then solves in one of two ways,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde u = G\,h(z),\;\; z\in\Real^{k}
  \quad(\text{head}),
  \qquad
  \tilde u = \widehat G_{R'}\,c,\;\; c\in\Real^{R'}
  \quad(\text{span}),
  \label{eq:modes}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat G_{R'}$ holds the first $R'\le R$ columns. The head is a
nonlinear manifold with $k$ unknowns; the span is a linear space with $R'$
unknowns. The width $R'$ is the accuracy knob. The columns are nested, so
narrowing the span needs no retraining, and a narrower span is cheaper both
to solve and to turn back into a field.

\subsection{Projecting the PDE onto the Manifold}
\label{sec:method:weak}

Each PDE we consider gives a discretised residual $R(u)$ that vanishes at
the discrete solution. We substitute the trial manifold into $R$ and,
instead of projecting with the decoder Jacobian, test it against $M$ fixed
vectors, the rows of $P\in\Real^{M\times N}$. On the square these are the
$M$ lowest sine modes, which are eigenvectors of $K$. A query solves
\begin{equation}
  z^{\star} = \argmin_{z}\;\tfrac12\,\norm{P\,R\big(\tilde u(z)\big)}_2^2,
  \qquad M>k,
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
and the span the same problem in $c$. This is least-squares
Petrov--Galerkin projection \citep{Carlberg2011LSPG,LeeCarlberg2020}; each
row of $PR$ is also scaled by a fixed factor that puts the tests on a
common scale (Appendix~\ref{app:method}). We solve \eqref{eq:objective} by
damped Gauss--Newton (Levenberg--Marquardt) \citep{Marquardt1963},
\begin{equation}
  \Delta z = -\big(J\T J+\lambda\,\diag(J\T J)\big)^{-1}J\T P\,R\big(\tilde u(z)\big),
  \qquad
  J = P\,\frac{\partial R}{\partial u}\,J_{\mathcal D}(z),
  \label{eq:lm}
\end{equation}
and keep a step only if the residual decreases. Here
$J_{\mathcal D}(z)=G\,J_h(z)$ for the head and $J_{\mathcal D}=\widehat
G_{R'}$, a constant, for the span. Unlike $J_{\mathcal D}(z)\T$, the tests
$P$ do not change with $z$, which is what lets every linear term be
computed once (\S\ref{sec:method:precomp}). For Poisson and heat in the
span, \eqref{eq:objective} is a linear least-squares problem solved once;
the iteration is needed only for the head and for the nonlinear PDEs.

\paragraph{Elliptic (Poisson).}
The Poisson residual is $R(u)=Ku-F$. In the span it is linear in $c$,
\begin{equation}
  P\,R\big(\widehat G_{R'}c\big) = \big(PK\widehat G_{R'}\big)\,c - PF,
  \label{eq:poisson-residual}
\end{equation}
so a query is one least-squares solve with a fixed $M\times R'$ matrix,
factorised once per mesh and width, and nothing is iterated. With the head,
\eqref{eq:lm} iterates in $z$ with $J=PKG\,J_h(z)$, and only the head is
re-evaluated.

\paragraph{Parabolic (heat).}
The semi-discretisation $du/dt=-\kappa Ku$ is advanced by Crank--Nicolson,
with the residual
\begin{equation}
  R(u_{n+1}) = \big(I+\tfrac{\Delta t\kappa}{2}K\big)u_{n+1}
             - \big(I-\tfrac{\Delta t\kappa}{2}K\big)u_{n}.
  \label{eq:heat-step}
\end{equation}
Each step solves
\begin{equation}
  z_{n+1} = \argmin_{z}\;\norm{P\,R\big(\tilde u(z)\big)}_2,
  \qquad u_n=\tilde u(z_n),
  \label{eq:step}
\end{equation}
by \eqref{eq:lm}, starting from $z_n$, and the span the same in $c_{n+1}$.
In the span the step is linear in $c_{n+1}$, so the rollout applies one
fixed $R'\times R'$ matrix per step.

\paragraph{Hyperbolic (Burgers).}
For $u_t+u(u_x+u_y)=\nu\Delta u$, with backward Euler and a sign-upwind
stencil (Appendix~\ref{app:method:burgers}), the residual is
\begin{equation}
  R(u_{n+1}) = u_{n+1}-u_{n}+\Delta t\big(A(u_{n+1})+\nu K u_{n+1}\big),
  \label{eq:burgers-step}
\end{equation}
where $A(u)$ is the advection term. The time derivative and diffusion are
linear in the state; advection is not, so both the head and the span
solve each step \eqref{eq:step} by iterating \eqref{eq:lm}, starting from
the previous step.

\paragraph{Incompressible flow (Navier--Stokes).}
For $u_t+(u\cdot\nabla)u=-\nabla p+\nu\Delta u$, $\nabla\cdot u=0$, on the
periodic cube, with the implicit midpoint rule, the residual is
\begin{equation}
  R(u_{n+1}) = u_{n+1}-u_{n}
  +\Delta t\big((\bar u\cdot\nabla)\bar u+\nabla\bar p-\nu\Delta\bar u\big),
  \qquad \bar u=\tfrac12(u_n+u_{n+1}).
  \label{eq:ns-step}
\end{equation}
The tests $P$ are divergence-free Fourier modes, so the pressure drops out
of $PR$. Advection is quadratic in the state, so in the bank it is a fixed
quadratic form of the coefficients, computed once, and needs no quadrature.
Both the head and the span solve each step \eqref{eq:step} by Gauss--Newton
\eqref{eq:lm}. The vortices travel across the domain, so the bank is
centred on the flow, and each step also solves for the three-number shift
of that centre (Appendix~\ref{app:method:ns}).

\subsection{Hyper-reduction}
\label{sec:method:precomp}

The manifold reduces the unknowns from $N$ to $k$ or $R'$, but the tested
residual $P\,R(\tilde u)$ still visits every node, so each evaluation costs
$O(NR)$, which kills the wall-clock benefit. We remove this cost in two
ways, one for linear terms and one for the nonlinear term.

\paragraph{Linear terms: computed once and exact.}
A linear term is tested once, offline. For the Laplacian,
\begin{equation}
  P K\tilde u = \big(PKG\big)\,h(z),
  \qquad PKG\in\Real^{M\times R},
  \label{eq:preassembled}
\end{equation}
and the span uses $PK\widehat G_{R'}$, the first $R'$ columns of
$PKG\,T$. Online a linear term costs $O(MR)$ and is exact. For Poisson and
heat this is the whole residual, so their reduced solves make no
approximation beyond the trial space. The quadratic term of Navier--Stokes
is also tested exactly, as a precomputed tensor.

\paragraph{The nonlinear term: Empirical Quadrature.}
Burgers advection cannot be computed once. We use Empirical Quadrature
\citep{Hernandez2017EQ,YanoPatera2019LPEQ} to select a non-negative
per-node weight vector $w$, independent of the state, such that
\begin{equation}
  P A(\tilde u) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} P_{:,i}\,A_i(\tilde u)
  \;\approx\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal S} w_i\,P_{:,i}\,A_i(\tilde u).
  \label{eq:eq-rule}
\end{equation}
Letting $g_i^{(\ell)}=P_{:,i}A_i(\tilde u^{(\ell)})\in\Real^{M}$ denote the
per-node contribution at a stored state $\tilde u^{(\ell)}$ that the solver
reaches on training trajectories, the constraint at that state is
$\sum_{i} w_i\,g_i^{(\ell)}=P A(\tilde u^{(\ell)})$. Stacking the
constraints gives a matrix $\mathcal G$ and a vector $b$, and we solve
\begin{equation}
  \min_{w\ge 0}\;\norm{\mathcal G\,w-b}_2
  \label{eq:nnls-main}
\end{equation}
by NNLS \citep{LawsonHanson1974NNLS}, which returns a sparse $w$ with
support $\mathcal S$ of size $N_{\mathrm{eq}}\ll N$. Computing $A_i$ needs
$\tilde u$ at node $i$ and its stencil neighbours $\mathcal N(i)$, so we
evaluate the decoder only on
$\mathcal S^{+}=\mathcal S\cup\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal S}\mathcal N(i)$, with
$|\mathcal S^{+}|\le 5N_{\mathrm{eq}}$. Because each bank column is a
coordinate network (\S\ref{sec:decoder}), those rows of $G$ are computed
once and cached.

\paragraph{Cost.}
With linear terms computed once and advection on $\mathcal S^{+}$, one
iteration of \eqref{eq:lm} costs
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal O\big(MR\,(N_{\mathrm{eq}}+k)\big),
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
with $k$ replaced by $R'$ in the span, independent of $N$. Returning the
field at an output time is one further $O(NR')$ product.

\paragraph{Checking the rule.}
A rule is an approximation, so we check it where it is used: on held-out
states that the reduced solver actually reaches. A rule is confirmed when
the relative error of the tested advection, $\rho$ in \eqref{eq:rho},
stays below a bar fixed in advance on every held-out draw and on one
further confirmation draw (Appendix~\ref{app:cg-current}). Every rule
behind Table~\ref{tab:headline} is confirmed. Backward Euler never
evaluates advection at the initial state. Our check as first registered
included that state; we removed it after seeing that it was the only state
at which the widest span settings failed, and this is the one change we
made to the check after seeing results. The rule changes how the residual
is evaluated, not what the model can represent, so it is a cost knob,
separate from $R'$.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:decoder}

Three properties of the solve motivate the model in
Figure~\ref{fig:decoder}. First, every head solve starts from a stored
training code and descends along the head's Jacobian, so that Jacobian
must keep a usable direction; this motivates a \emph{linear skip} in the
head. Second, empirical quadrature keeps a sparse set of nodes and
discards the rest, which saves time only if the decoder can be evaluated
at one node without the others, and one trained model must serve every
mesh of a series; this motivates a \emph{coordinate-network bank}. Third,
one trained model must offer several accuracy settings without
retraining; this motivates an \emph{ordered bank}. Widths and training
settings are in Appendix~\ref{app:training-schedule}.

\paragraph{Linear skip, for the latent solve.}
The head is a shallow network plus a linear map,
\begin{equation}
  \head(\latent) \;=\; W_{\mathrm{dir}}\latent \;+\; \varphi_\theta(\latent),
  \qquad
  \varphi_\theta(\latent)=W_3\,\sigma\big(W_2\,\sigma(W_1\latent+b_1)+b_2\big)+b_3,
  \label{eq:head}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma$ the SiLU activation. Its Jacobian is
$\dhead(\latent)=W_{\mathrm{dir}}+J_{\varphi}(\latent)$. The first term does not
depend on $\latent$, so the Levenberg--Marquardt step from a stored code
always has a fixed linear direction to follow, while $\varphi_\theta$
supplies the curvature of the manifold. The head acts on the $R$ bank
coefficients, not on the $n$ nodes, so its cost and its parameter count do
not depend on the mesh.

\paragraph{Coordinate-network bank, for node-local evaluation.}
Each column of the bank is a coordinate network read at the node and
multiplied by the boundary factor of \eqref{eq:bank},
\begin{equation}
  \bank_{x,r}=\bcmask(x)\,g_{\phi,r}(x),
  \qquad
  g_\phi(x)=\mathrm{MLP}_\phi\big(\gamma(x)\big),
  \quad
  \gamma(x)=\big[\cos(2\pi\Omega x),\;\sin(2\pi\Omega x)\big],
  \label{eq:coordnet}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is a random-Fourier-feature embedding with a fixed random
frequency matrix $\Omega$ \citep{Tancik2020FourierFeatures}. Because
$g_\phi$ reads a coordinate, a row of $\bank$ is computed from its node
alone. A quadrature rule therefore needs only the rows of $\mathcal S^{+}$,
and the same trained network is sampled on every mesh. Two natural
alternatives fail one of these tests. A convolutional or attention
decoder couples its output nodes, so one node costs a pass over the whole
grid, and a learned dense readout stores $NR$ parameters and must be
retrained for each mesh. The parameter count of $g_\phi$ does not depend
on $n$.

\paragraph{Ordered bank, for deployment-time accuracy.}
The rotation \eqref{eq:ordered} puts the most useful directions first.
Cutting the bank at $R'$ then gives the best $R'$-dimensional subspace of
the bank for the training fields in the least-squares sense, and the
settings are nested, so one model built with $R$ columns contains every
smaller one. A query picks $R'$ and no weight changes. The two solve modes
of \eqref{eq:modes} are the two ends of the model. The span is a linear
ROM in a learned bank, and on a linear PDE it is one least-squares solve.
The head is a nonlinear-manifold ROM in $k$ unknowns and can follow
states that no small span contains. The alternatives, a separate model
per accuracy or a retrained smaller bank, both need retraining. One frozen
model supplies both settings of every row of Table~\ref{tab:headline}.

and the stopping rule, and leaves the weights fixed.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

\subsection{Matrix-free Projected Operators via JAX}

The matrix $PKG$ of \S\ref{sec:method:precomp} is built once per mesh, so
the online value of a linear term is one small matrix--vector product. In
the span the Jacobian is the fixed matrix $PK\widehat G_{R'}$. With the
head the remaining derivative is the Jacobian of the head, which
forward-mode automatic differentiation \citep{Bradbury2018JAX} supplies,
\begin{equation}
  J_h\, v \;=\; \mathrm{JVP}(h,z,v),
  \qquad
  J \;=\; PKG\,J_h(z).
  \label{eq:jvp}
\end{equation}
Each accepted step solves the damped normal equation directly. A linear
PDE does not return to the grid, and the projected operator is not passed
to a Krylov method. Burgers advection reads the cached stencil on the
quadrature nodes, and its Jacobian is assembled analytically
(Appendix~\ref{app:method:burgers}). The ordered bank is stored in column
blocks at the tested widths, so a query at width $R'$ reads exactly $R'$
columns.

\subsection{Training Protocol}

Training is an auto-decoder on training data only, in two stages. The
coordinate network $g_\phi$ is fitted to the training states through
\eqref{eq:bank} and frozen, which fixes the bank. The head is then fitted
to the bank coefficients of those states, with one stored code per
training state. Last, the bank is ordered once by \eqref{eq:ordered}. This
step has no trainable weights and takes one QR and one SVD. Widths,
cohorts and offline costs are in Appendix~\ref{app:training-schedule}.

\section{Experimental Setup and Benchmark Problems}
\label{sec:setup}
\restoredisplays

\paragraph{Problems and references.}
In two dimensions we solve Poisson on the square, heat and viscous
Burgers, with an L-shaped Poisson domain as an ill-conditioned check. In
three dimensions we solve Poisson, heat, viscous Burgers and incompressible
Navier--Stokes on the periodic cube, whose initial velocity is the curl of
two localised vector potentials with random centre, separation, strength
and viscosity. Meshes and cohorts are in Table~\ref{tab:problems}, the
sampled families in Table~\ref{tab:spec} and the three-dimensional
configurations in Table~\ref{tab:config3d}.

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
We report the worst relative $L^2$ error over the stated cases and
requested times, in percent. Poisson uses the steady reference norm and
heat the current reference norm; Burgers and Navier--Stokes use the
initial-field norm over evolved times. Same-grid error measures the
reduction error against the converged discrete solution. Error against a
refined reference also includes discretisation error, and we never
interchange the two. Settings for a held-out cohort are fixed before its
cases are opened; all other cohorts are development cohorts.

\paragraph{Timing and speedup.}
Every accuracy--runtime pair comes from the same solver invocation, in
double precision, with GPU burn-in, synchronised timing and medians of
retained repetitions (protocol in Appendix~\ref{app:cg-current}). GPU
query time covers initialisation, the solve or rollout and the requested
device outputs. Training and compilation are offline costs. Speedup is
$S=T_{\mathrm{FOM}}/T_{\mathrm{method}}$ with both times from the same
allocation, and each table names the FOM and shows its error. Solves that
miss their stopping rule are reported as such and do not enter a
speedup.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Poisson, including the L-shaped check, is compared with conjugate
gradients (CG), and heat with Crank--Nicolson stepping whose linear solve
is CG (CN--CG). Burgers is compared with Newton--BiCGStab. Navier--Stokes
is compared with the solver that generated its data, a Fourier
pseudo-spectral method with Crank--Nicolson diffusion and Adams--Bashforth
advection (CNAB2), which needs no linear solve. The named FOM of a row is
that solver at its fastest tested setting that is at least as accurate as
the accurate NM-ROM setting. Other nonlinear-manifold ROMs are compared in
Table~\ref{tab:nmrom-baselines}, and neural operators (FNO, U-Net,
DeepONet and Transolver) in \S\ref{sec:results:neuralops}.

\paragraph{Linear problems.}
On Poisson both settings of a row are solves in the span of the ordered
bank. At $4096^2$ the accurate width $R'=512$ reaches
$\nBwPoisAccErr\,\%$ at $\nBwPoisAccS\times$ the speed of CG, and the fast
width $R'=128$ reaches $\nBwPoisFastErr\,\%$ at $\nBwPoisFastS\times$. The
head is less accurate here ($\nBwPoisHeadErr\,\%$): on a linear problem
the best solve in the bank is the linear one, and it is also the cheapest.
The speedup grows with the mesh because the reduced query does not visit
the grid until it returns the field. On the L-shaped check at $2048^2$
the accurate setting is $\nHeadLshapeAccS\times$ faster than CG at
$\nHeadLshapeAccErr\,\%$. A
wide heat bank, evaluated on a held-out cohort never used for selection,
reaches $\nHeatWideAccErr\,\%$ over all times in the span of its first
128 ordered functions and is $\nHeatWideBatchedAccS\times$ faster than
CN--CG at $2048^2$. In three dimensions the held-out Poisson cohort gives
$\nHeadPoissonThreeAccErr\,\%$ at $\nHeadPoissonThreeAccS\times$ at $64^3$. The
three-dimensional heat model, evaluated on a held-out cohort of
\nHeatNewCases{} cases, reaches 0.083\,\% over all times in the span of its
first 320 ordered functions and is 7.60$\times$ faster than CN--CG at
$256^3$; the span of 128 reaches 1.11\,\% at 16.8$\times$. As on Poisson,
the span is both more accurate and cheaper than the head at every mesh.

\paragraph{Burgers.}
At coarse meshes the reduced solve is slower than Newton--BiCGStab, but its
cost barely changes with the mesh. The fast setting (the span of the first
128 ordered functions) overtakes the FOM by $512^2$ (1.59$\times$ at
1.75\,\%) and is 21.7$\times$ faster at $4096^2$. The accurate setting (the
span of 384) reaches 0.17--0.22\,\% at every mesh and is faster than the
FOM from $1024^2$, $\nBurgDevAccSFortyNinetySix\times$ at $4096^2$. Every
quadrature rule in these rows is confirmed on the states its residual
evaluates (\S\ref{sec:method:precomp}). On 64 held-out cases the same
settings reach a worst error of 0.88--1.35\,\% (median 0.03\,\%) for the
accurate setting and 2.55--3.11\,\% (median 0.27--0.30\,\%) for the fast
one at $256^2$--$1024^2$ and $4096^2$, at 0.38--10.6$\times$ and
0.94--27.3$\times$ the speed of Newton--BiCGStab in the same jobs: a few
unseen cases are harder than the development cases, while the typical
case is not.

In three dimensions one frozen span model, trained on $32^3$--$128^3$
fields and used unchanged at $256^3$, is tested on 32 sealed held-out
cases with settings chosen on 64 validation cases. At $256^3$ the span of
512 reaches 2.20\,\% at 2.19$\times$ the speed of Newton--BiCGStab and the
span of 256 reaches 3.77\,\% at 5.60$\times$. The fast setting is also
faster at $64^3$ and $128^3$ (2.08$\times$ and 5.10$\times$), but there the
accurate setting is slower than the FOM, whose cost only grows sharply
beyond $128^3$. The advection term here is a precomputed quadratic form of
the coefficients, confirmed on reached states like the quadrature rules
($\rho\le 2\times10^{-3}$). The nonlinear head did not generalise on this
family and is not used.

\paragraph{Navier--Stokes.}
Here the head earns its place. At $96^3$ the head with $k=\nNsHeadK$
reaches $\nNsHeadErr\,\%$ and is $\nNsHeadS\times$ faster than CNAB2 at
matched accuracy. The span of the same bank is
$\nNsHeadVsSpan\times$ less accurate at the same number of unknowns, and
even the full rank-$\nNsBankR$ span reaches only $\nNsSpanWideErr\,\%$.
The moving vortices lie on a low-dimensional curved manifold that the
head follows and a small linear span does not. The error stays flat from
$32^3$ to $96^3$ while the speedup rises from $\nNsSmallS\times$ to
$\nNsHeadS\times$. On \nNsHoldCases{} held-out cases the frozen settings
give $\nNsHoldHeadErr\,\%$ at $\nNsHoldHeadS\times$ (8.01$\times$ when re-timed alongside the operators, Table~\ref{tab:operators}).

\paragraph{Where the speedup comes from.}
On the linear problems the query does not iterate. In the span the tested
residual is linear in $c$, so the query is one least-squares solve with a
matrix factorised offline, and for heat each time step is a fixed matrix
applied to $c$. The query time is then almost entirely reading the input
and writing the output field, which costs $O(NR')$: on Poisson at
$4096^2$, rebuilding the field takes 41.8 of the 42.4\,ms, while CG needs
about 5300 iterations over all nodes. On the nonlinear problems the query
iterates, but the count does not grow with the mesh. Burgers takes about
one Levenberg--Marquardt iteration per time step (54 over 50 steps for the
accurate setting at every mesh from $1024^2$ to $4096^2$), and
Navier--Stokes a fixed three Gauss--Newton sweeps per step. Each iteration
costs \eqref{eq:cost}, independent of $N$, while the full-order solvers
sweep all $N$ nodes in every sparse matrix--vector product and, for CG,
need more of them as the mesh is refined \citep{Saad2003}. For a fixed error this is plain in Figure~\ref{fig:cost-scaling}: refining the Burgers mesh 256-fold raises our query time less than twofold and the solver's about thirtyfold, and above $1024^2$ our model is cheaper at every target. Fitting time $\propto N^p$ at a fixed error gives $p\approx0.1$ for our model on Burgers and $0.4$ on Navier--Stokes, against about $0.65$ and $1.0$ for the full-order solvers; on heat, where only reading and writing the field grows, $p\approx0.9$ against $1.2$. So in six of
the eight series of Table~\ref{tab:headline} (Poisson, heat and Burgers in
two dimensions, Poisson, Burgers and Navier--Stokes in three) the speedup rises at
every refinement while the error stays near its coarse-mesh value
(Table~\ref{tab:iterations}). On the L-shape it dips at $512^2$ and then
rises to 84.2$\times$ at $2048^2$; on three-dimensional heat it stays
between 5.79$\times$ and 10.9$\times$ for the accurate setting
(12.7--16.8$\times$ fast) at 0.080--0.083\,\% error.

\subsection{Which Knob to Turn}\label{sec:results:tunability}\label{sec:results:best}

Every measurement behind this section, including the full ladders, is in
Table~\ref{tab:tunability} in the appendix. None of these settings
retrains a weight.

\paragraph{Accuracy: the bank width and the head.}
The bank width $R'$ changes what the trained model can represent, so it is
the accuracy knob. On Poisson at $4096^2$, narrowing the bank from
$R'=512$ to $32$ raises the error $\nBwPoisErrSpan\times$, from
$\nBwPoisAccErr$ to $\nBwPoisNarrowErr\,\%$, and lowers the query time
$\nBwPoisCostSpan\times$, so the same model moves from $\nBwPoisAccS\times$
to $\nBwPoisNarrowS\times$ the speed of CG. On heat at $2048^2$ the span
runs from 0.13\,\% at 216$\times$ ($R'=128$) to 1.08\,\% at 512$\times$
($R'=32$), and on Burgers at $4096^2$ from 0.22\,\% at 8.78$\times$
($R'=384$) to 10.7\,\% at 43.2$\times$ ($R'=32$). On Navier--Stokes the
span runs from $\nNsSpanWideErr\,\%$ at $R'=64$ to $\nNsSpanNarrowErr\,\%$
at $R'=8$, at $\nNsSpanCostSpan\times$ lower cost. The choice between head
and span depends on the problem. On Poisson and heat the span wins at every
width, so the knob is $R'$ alone. On Navier--Stokes the head is the
accurate end: at the same number of unknowns it is $\nNsHeadVsSpan\times$
more accurate than the span, and costs about twice as much, because each
step also evaluates the network and its Jacobian. This is what makes the
NM-ROM tunable in a way a trained neural operator is not: the same frozen
model is reused, and only a deployment argument changes.

\paragraph{Limitations.}\label{sec:limitations}
\label{sec:results:head}\label{sec:results:collapse}\label{sec:exp:eq}
(i) Except for Navier--Stokes, speedups are against named iterative
full-order solvers at the stated tolerances. Fast direct and spectral
solvers exist for the square and the cube and would be faster than CG
there. The named FOM is more accurate than the NM-ROM in every row.
(ii) The Navier--Stokes bank is a POD basis in a moving frame, not a
coordinate network, and the family has moderate Reynolds numbers; we make
no claim about turbulent flow.
(iii) On Poisson the errors lie near the bank floor
($\nHiresFloorSquare\,\%$ square, $\nHiresFloorCube\,\%$ cube), so a wider
or better bank, not the solver, limits accuracy. On the L-shape
($\nHiresLshapeAccErr\,\%$ against a full-bank floor of
$\nHiresLshapeFloor\,\%$) the span cannot be wider than 128 with the
available tests, so more tests, not the bank, limit accuracy.
(iv) Quadrature validation covers finitely many reached states and is not
a global certificate, and we changed the check once after seeing results
(\S\ref{sec:method:precomp}); no rule is used in three dimensions. Reduced
solves can converge to a wrong branch, and lower same-grid error does not
remove discretisation error.
(v) The evaluation cohorts are small (6--64 cases). Timings report medians without dispersion.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

We built an NM-ROM whose distinguishing property is a deployment-time
accuracy/speed tradeoff from a single trained model: at inference, the
width of the ordered bank, the choice between the head and the span, and
on the nonlinear problems the quadrature rule and the stopping tolerance
trade accuracy for speed. This
is the lever neural operators do not expose, since each trained FNO or
DeepONet delivers one fixed operating point at a given mesh. Mechanically, the decoder is
a learned manifold built from a coordinate-network bank and a nonlinear
head; the PDE itself is enforced by least-squares Petrov--Galerkin
projection of the discrete residual onto that manifold; Dirichlet
boundary conditions are enforced exactly by the bank; linear terms are
preassembled exactly and empirical quadrature removes the mesh from the
nonlinear term; and the whole pipeline is matrix-free in JAX.

Three pieces had to come together to make this work. First, a
coordinate-network bank, which is evaluated at one node without the
others and serves every mesh of a series from one trained network.
Second, a one-time ordering of that bank, which turns one trained model
into a nested family of trial spaces chosen at the query. Third, a head
with a linear skip, which gives the latent solve a usable direction from a
stored code and supplies the accurate end where a linear span is not
enough, as on Navier--Stokes.

The model keeps its accuracy as the mesh is refined, so its speedup over
the named solvers grows with resolution: at a fixed error its query time
grows as about $N^{0.1}$ on Burgers and $N^{0.4}$ on Navier--Stokes,
against $N^{0.65}$ and $N^{1.0}$ for the solvers. On viscous Burgers at $4096^2$ it
reaches $\nBurgDevAccErrFortyNinetySix\,\%$ at
$\nBurgDevAccSFortyNinetySix\times$ the speed of Newton--BiCGStab, and on
three-dimensional Navier--Stokes at $96^3$ the head reaches
$\nNsHeadErr\,\%$ at $\nNsHeadS\times$ the speed of a spectral solver,
$\nNsHeadVsSpan\times$ more accurate than a linear span of the same size.
On the linear problems the ordered span is enough: $\nBwPoisAccErr\,\%$ on
Poisson at $\nBwPoisAccS\times$ the speed of CG at $4096^2$.
A natural next step is a
coordinate-network bank for moving-frame flows, quadrature in three
dimensions, and advection-dominated three-dimensional problems.

This appendix gives the details of \S\ref{sec:method} that the main text
leaves out: the ordering of the bank, the row scaling of each PDE, the
Burgers stencil, the Navier--Stokes family, the quadrature fit and the
solver constants. On the square the tests are eigenvectors of the
Laplacian, $PK=\Lambda P$ with $\Lambda$ diagonal, and every PDE scales the
tested residual $PR$ by the inverse of a diagonal matrix built from
$\Lambda$. We write $\rweak$ for the scaled tested residual.

\subsection{Ordering the bank}
\label{app:method:ordered}
Let $G=Q_GR_G$ be a thin QR factorisation. For every training field $u_i$
we take its bank coefficients $a_i$ and stack the scaled vectors
$R_Ga_i/\norm{u_i}$ as the rows of a matrix $\mathcal A$. With the singular
value decomposition $\mathcal A=USV\T$ we set $T=R_G^{-1}V$ in
\eqref{eq:ordered}. The columns of $\widehat G=GT$ are then orthonormal and
ordered by the singular values in $S$. Because $T$ is invertible, the
full-width bank gives the same trial states as before (to $10^{-10}$ in our
runs), and the head is kept unchanged. The step uses training data only and
has no trainable weights.

\subsection{Poisson}
\label{app:method:poisson}
The tested residual is scaled by $\Lambda^{-1}$. Because the retained
tests are orthonormal and $Ku^{\star}=F$, the scaled residual
$\Lambda^{-1}P(K\tilde u-F)=P(\tilde u-u^{\star})$ is exactly the tested
error, so the objective is the squared discrete $L^2$ error of the reduced
state in the retained modes. In the span, the thin QR factorisation of
$P\widehat G_{R'}$ is computed once per mesh and width, and a query is one
matrix--vector product and one triangular solve. At $R'=R$
the span solve reaches the bank floor in the tested norm.

\subsection{Heat}
\label{app:method:heat}
The tested Crank--Nicolson residual \eqref{eq:heat-step} is scaled by
$(I+\tfrac{\Delta t\kappa}{2}\Lambda)^{-1}$, which turns each step into
$P\tilde u_{n+1}=D\,P\tilde u_{n}$ with
$D=\diag\big((1-\tfrac{\Delta t\kappa}{2}\lambda_i)/(1+\tfrac{\Delta t\kappa}{2}\lambda_i)\big)$:
each tested mode is multiplied by its Crank--Nicolson factor. In the
span the step is the linear recurrence
\begin{equation}
  c_{n+1}=B_{R'}^{+}D\,B_{R'}\,c_n,
  \label{eq:heat-endpoint}
\end{equation}
with $B_{R'}=P\widehat G_{R'}$ and $c_0$ fitted to the initial field.
Because the step matrix $B_{R'}^{+}D\,B_{R'}$ is fixed, its powers at the
output times are formed once offline, so a query fits $c_0$, applies one
small matrix per output time and rebuilds the fields. This is exactly the
Crank--Nicolson solution of the reduced problem at the full-order time step
$\Delta t=0.025$, and every heat result in the paper is computed this
way.

\subsection{Burgers}
\label{app:method:burgers}
The full-order problem uses the sign-upwind stencil
\begin{equation}
  A(u)_{ij} \;=\; u_{ij}\,\big(\delta_x u + \delta_y u\big)_{ij},
  \qquad
  (\delta_x u)_{ij} = \frac{1}{\Delta x}
  \begin{cases}
    u_{ij}-u_{i-1,j}, & u_{ij}>0,\\[2pt]
    u_{i+1,j}-u_{ij}, & u_{ij}\le 0,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:upwind}
\end{equation}
with $\delta_y$ analogous, zero ghost values on the walls and backward Euler
in time. The tested residual \eqref{eq:burgers-step} is scaled by
$(I+\Delta t\,\nu\Lambda)^{-1}$, the modal form of the Helmholtz
preconditioner the full-order Newton solver uses. Each step is
warm-started from the previous coefficients, and a linear or quadratic
extrapolation is used when its residual is smaller. At every mesh the step
assembles the Jacobian analytically, clips the step, solves the damped
normal equation by Cholesky factorisation and carries the damping between
steps; the rows at $256^2$--$1024^2$ also apply the test projections
separably and, where faster, run as CUDA graphs, which reproduces the
iterates to $6\times10^{-12}$ with identical iteration counts.

\subsection{Navier--Stokes}
\label{app:method:ns}
The unknown is the three-component, zero-mean velocity on the periodic unit
cube. The initial velocity is the curl of two localised periodic vector
potentials, each of the form $\exp[2\sum_{i}(\cos(2\pi(x_i-c_i))-1)]$ along
a fixed direction, with random common centre, separation in $[0.12,0.24]$,
relative strength in $[0.6,1.4]$ and viscosity log-uniform in
$[0.002,0.01]$; the horizon is $T=0.2$ with six output times. The data and
the full-order comparator come from CNAB2 with two-thirds dealiasing. The
bank is a rank-64 POD of training states, each centred on its own energy
centroid; the centroid moves with the vortices, and a query solves for its
three-component shift at every step together with the latent coordinates. All reduced tensors, including the quadratic term, are computed
offline, so no quadrature is needed. The head is trained as an
auto-decoder on the centred bank coefficients of training states. The span
uses the first $R'$ columns of this bank ordered by importance. The tests
are the $M=292$ lowest divergence-free Fourier modes. A query centres the
initial field, projects it on the bank and, for the head, starts from a stored training code. It then takes 10 implicit-midpoint steps of
$\Delta t=0.02$, each solved by three damped Gauss--Newton sweeps with an
analytic Jacobian, starting from a linear extrapolation of the last two
steps. With the centring held fixed the same bank
misses every case by far more than 5\,\%, so the centring is
necessary.

\subsection{Empirical quadrature}
\label{app:method:eq}
On one state, a rule's error is
\begin{equation}
  \rho(u)=\frac{\norm{\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}w_iP_{:,i}A_i(u)-PA(u)}_2}{\norm{PA(u)}_2},
  \qquad \rho_{\max}\le\nEqtopBar ,
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
over held-out reachable states. The bar was fixed before any check
was run. The fit \eqref{eq:nnls-main} normalises each row, grows the
support greedily by the Lawson--Hanson criterion with an exact
non-negative refit after each addition, and stops at $N_{\mathrm{eq}}$ nodes. The fit
states are states the dense solver reaches on fit trajectories; the
held-out states come from trajectories disjoint from the fit and the
evaluation cases. A deployed rule is its node indices, weights and the
cached $N_{\mathrm{eq}}\times5\times R$ bank block. The Burgers rows of
Table~\ref{tab:headline} use a rule that needs no fit: the uniform
interior lattice with 64 intervals per axis, $63\times63=3969$ nodes with
equal weights, which are the same physical points on every mesh. It is
checked on reached states in the same way as a fitted rule.

\subsection{Solver constants}
\label{app:method:codes}
Each Levenberg--Marquardt attempt solves
$(H+\lambda\,\diag(\diag H))\,\delta=-g$ with $H=\jac{}\T\jac{}$ and
$g=\jac{}\T\rweak$, and accepts $\delta$ only if the residual decreases.
The stopping test is scale free,
$\norm{\jac{}\T\rweak}_2\le\eta_{\mathrm{tol}}\normF{\jac{}}\,\norm{\rweak}_2$,
so one tolerance serves every mesh and width. Damping starts at
$\lambda_0=10^{-6}$ (Poisson, Burgers) or $10^{-4}$ (heat), is divided by 3
on acceptance and multiplied by 10 on rejection. Head solves start from a stored training code. A solve that stalls is reported
as early-stopped, separately from one that meets the stopping test. The
\emph{bank floor} is the
projection error onto the span of the bank, which no solve can beat.

\paragraph{How much data each side was given.}
On Burgers the operators were trained on \nParOpTraj{} trajectories and
our model on \nParRomTraj{}. The gap comes from the cost of one sample, not from a
choice. Both sides draw initial conditions from the same family and are
scored at the same output times, but at $256^2$ each operator target is a
$4096$-interval solution restricted to the $256$-interval grid, which
costs about $\nParCostRatio$ times more than a trajectory behind our bank
and head. At $1024^2$ and $2048^2$ the same \nParOpTraj{} trajectories are
recomputed on the evaluation mesh with a tight Newton tolerance, so there
the operators are trained on exactly the fields they are scored against,
while our model, trained at $256^2$, is used unchanged. Matching \nParRomTraj{} trajectories at that fidelity would
cost about $\nParFullHours$\,A100-hours. An operator also sees one
input--output pair per trajectory (\nParOpStates{} supervised states),
while the head is fitted per state (\nParRomStates{} states).

\subsection{Headline settings, times and timing protocol}
\label{app:cg-current}\label{app:main-experiment-details}
The settings behind Table~\ref{tab:headline}, all from one frozen model
per problem with no retraining: Poisson (square and cube) and heat use the
bank span at two widths ($R'=512/128$ square, $128/64$ cube, $128/48$ heat
2D, $320/128$ heat 3D, Crank--Nicolson stepping); Navier--Stokes uses the
head $k=8$ and the span $R'=16$ (the cheapest span setting below 5\,\%);
the L-shape uses the span $R'=128/64$ (the widest span its tests support is
128); Burgers uses the span $R'=384$ and $R'=128$ at every mesh. Burgers 3D uses
one coordinate bank ($R=512$, trained once on $32^3$, $64^3$ and $128^3$
fields) with the span $R'=512$ and $R'=192$ ($R'=256$ at $256^3$), a
fixed-sweep Levenberg--Marquardt solve and a precomputed quadratic advection
tensor (about $4R'$ tests); its settings were chosen by the same rule on 64
validation cases and its rows are 32 sealed held-out cases, all on H200
GPUs. At $128^3$ the FOM setting chosen on validation is less accurate than
the accurate setting on the held-out cases, so the rule picks a tighter
Newton tolerance; against the validation choice (27.4\,ms) the fast
setting is 2.87$\times$ faster. The Burgers $4096^2$ row was timed on an
H200 GPU and the other Burgers rows on A100 80\,GB GPUs; the reduced query
barely depends on the mesh, so the faster GPU puts $4096^2$ below
$2048^2$.
Over evolved times the accurate three-dimensional heat error is
$\nHeatNewBatchedAccEvolved\,\%$. At $128^3$ and $256^3$ the three-dimensional
Poisson rows use 16 development cases. The FOM
setting can differ between meshes of a series.
