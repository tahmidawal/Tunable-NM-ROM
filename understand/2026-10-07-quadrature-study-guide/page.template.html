Quadrature, step by step

The rules in Hari's off-mesh study — Gauss, rank-1 lattices (Fibonacci and CBC), Sobol, Smolyak and Monte Carlo — and why they matter for our reduced model.

Every number and figure here is produced by exercises.py (in understand/2026-10-07-quadrature-study-guide/), using an unchanged copy of Hari's rule code. The 2D test integrand is an illustrative smooth bump that behaves like ours; our real bank is harder and needs more points, so these are teaching numbers, not paper numbers.

Step 0The one problem every rule solves

Every time step, our reduced model needs, for each sine test function $\psi_{ab}(x,y)=\sin(a\pi x)\sin(b\pi y)$,

$$N_{ab}=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 f\,dx\,dy,\qquad f=\psi_{ab}\,u\,(u_x+u_y),\qquad u=G(x,y)\,c .$$

We cannot integrate exactly, so we pick $m$ points $x_q$ and weights $w_q$:

$$\int f\;\approx\;\sum_{q=1}^{m} w_q\,f(x_q).$$

That is all a quadrature rule is: a list of points and weights. The rules differ only in where the points go and how they are weighted. Three facts about our integrand decide which rule wins:

  • it is very smooth (the bank is an analytic neural network);
  • it vanishes at the walls (the field and each sine test are zero on the boundary);
  • it oscillates, because the tests go up to frequencies of roughly 25–45.

Step 1Three ways to integrate in 1D

  • Equal spacing (midpoint rule): $x_k=(k+\tfrac12)/n$, all weights $1/n$. This is the 1D version of a lattice.
  • Monte Carlo: $n$ random points, weights $1/n$.
  • Gauss–Legendre: $p$ special points (roots of the Legendre polynomial $P_p$) with special weights. It is the unique $p$-point rule that is exact for every polynomial of degree up to $2p-1$.

Check Gauss's promise. A degree-5 polynomial needs $2p-1\ge 5$, so $p=3$:

{{t1_gauss_poly}}

At $p=3$ the error drops to rounding level, exactly as promised.

Look at the right panel. When the integrand vanishes at both ends, equal spacing suddenly gets much better. Doubling the points cuts the error by:

{{t1_uniform_ratio}}

A ratio of 4 means error $\propto h^2$; a ratio of 16 means error $\propto h^4$. Vanishing at the ends removes the “seam” when the function is wrapped around periodically, and equal spacing is excellent for periodic functions.

Lesson
  • Gauss is the champion for smooth functions: its error falls geometrically.
  • Equal spacing is mediocre in general, but very good when the integrand vanishes at the ends.
  • Monte Carlo is slow everywhere: error falls only like $1/\sqrt n$.

Step 2Oscillation: Gauss must first “see” the wiggles

Multiply a smooth bump by $\sin(a\pi x)$ and integrate with Gauss:

{{t2_onset}}

Nothing happens until $p$ resolves the oscillation (roughly $p\gtrsim a$); then the error collapses.

What this means for our ROM

Our tests reach $a,b\approx 25$–$45$, so a Gauss rule needs at least that many points per direction before it works. In 3D that is $p^3$ points — why Gauss $16^3$ failed on our 3D model and $24^3$–$32^3$ worked.

Step 3Going to 2D: five ways to place points

Tensor Gauss

Take the 1D Gauss rule in each direction and form all pairs: $x_q=(x_i,x_j)$, $w_q=w_iw_j$. That is $p^2$ points in 2D and $p^3$ in 3D — geometric convergence, but the point count is cubed in 3D.

Rank-1 lattice (Fibonacci in 2D, CBC in 3D)

Choose an integer vector $z$ and a random shift $\Delta$, equal weights:

$$x_k=\Big\{\tfrac{k\,z}{m}+\Delta\Big\},\qquad w_k=\tfrac1m,\qquad k=0,\dots,m-1,$$

where $\{\cdot\}$ keeps the fractional part (wraps around the square). The points form a tilted, perfectly even grid.

  • Fibonacci (2D): $m=F_k$ and $z=(1,F_{k-1})$, e.g. $m=233$, $z=(1,144)$ — the best possible lattice in 2D.
  • CBC (3D and up): “component by component”: pick $z_1=1$, then the $z_2$ that minimises an error measure, then $z_3$ given those, and so on. Cheap to build, close to optimal in any dimension.

Sobol (quasi-Monte Carlo)

A deterministic sequence built from binary digits so that every small box gets its fair share of points (“low discrepancy”). Weights $1/m$. “Scrambled” randomly permutes digits, keeping the evenness and making error estimates unbiased.

Smolyak sparse grid

Instead of the full $p\times p$ grid, add up small tensor grids at different resolutions, with plus and minus signs:

$$A(q,d)=\sum_{q-d+1\le|\mathbf i|\le q}(-1)^{q-|\mathbf i|}\binom{d-1}{q-|\mathbf i|}\,Q_{i_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes Q_{i_d}.$$

Fine resolution along one axis is only combined with coarse resolution along the other, so the points form a cross. Far fewer than $p^d$ points, but some weights are negative.

Monte Carlo

Random points, weights $1/m$ — the baseline every other rule should beat.

Step 4The race on an integrand like ours

Integrand $f=\psi_{ab}\,u\,(u_x+u_y)$ with a smooth bump $u$ that vanishes on the walls. Error is divided by $\int|f|$, the size of the integrand. Two tests: a gentle $\psi_{3,4}$ and a mixed high-frequency $\psi_{12,12}$.

{{t4_at4096}}
Lesson
  • Gauss and the lattice are orders of magnitude ahead — both exploit smoothness.
  • Sobol and Monte Carlo plateau — they don't exploit smoothness at all.
  • Smolyak is fine on the gentle test and far worse on $\psi_{12,12}$. A sparse grid keeps detail in one direction at a time; a test that is high-frequency in both directions is exactly what it throws away.

Step 5Why the lattice works: aliasing

Write $f$ as a Fourier series $f(x)=\sum_h \hat f(h)e^{2\pi i h\cdot x}$. A lattice sum gets every frequency exactly right except those it cannot tell apart from zero, the “dual lattice” $h\cdot z\equiv 0 \pmod m$:

$$\frac1m\sum_k f(x_k)-\int f=\sum_{\substack{h\ne0\\ h\cdot z\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)}}\hat f(h)\,e^{2\pi i h\cdot\Delta}.$$
  • A good $z$ (Fibonacci or CBC) pushes those aliased frequencies out to very high $h$.
  • A smooth integrand has tiny Fourier coefficients there, so the error is tiny.
  • Vanishing at the walls makes the wrapped function nearly seamless, so its coefficients decay fast (Step 1 again).
  • The remaining kink at the walls gives an algebraic rate, not Gauss's geometric one — but it does not worsen with dimension, while Gauss needs $p^d$ points. That is why lattices beat Gauss in 3D.

Step 6The tent transform, and why it does not help here

Lattices are designed for periodic functions. The textbook fix for a non-periodic integrand is the tent map $x\mapsto 1-|2x-1|$, which folds the square so the function becomes periodic. Ours already vanishes at the walls, so the tent only doubles the frequencies the rule must resolve:

{{t6_tent}}
What this means for our ROM

Hari saw the same: the tent always hurt. His explanation (“it adds a kink”) is wrong for this integrand — the tent actually makes it smoother; the loss comes from the doubled frequencies. The paper will carry this correction.

Step 7The twist: the mesh's answer is not the true answer

The full-order solver does not compute $\int f$. It computes a mesh sum with a first-order upwind difference:

$$h^2\sum_{\text{nodes } i}\psi_{ab}(x_i)\,u_i\,(D^{\mathrm{up}}u)_i,\qquad D^{\mathrm{up}}u\approx u_x+u_y+O(h).$$
{{t7_gap}}

The gap halves with every mesh doubling: an $O(h)$ discretisation error.

  • The old methods (mesh sub-lattice, fitted EQ, precomputed tensor) all reproduce the mesh sum, gap included.
  • An off-mesh rule integrates the true $\int f$ with the bank's exact gradient, so the gap never enters.
What this means for our ROM

This is why, on the 3D model, the old tensor's error shrank with the mesh while the off-mesh error stayed the same at every mesh. It is also why the paper needs second-order references: part of the gain may simply be that the first-order mesh sum is crude.

Step 8Putting it into the reduced model

  1. Offline, once: decode the bank and its gradient at the $m$ rule points, $B=G(x_q)$ and $D=\partial G(x_q)$, each $m\times R'$.
  2. Online, every iteration: $u(x_q)=Bc$, $(u_x+u_y)(x_q)=Dc$; multiply pointwise, weight by $w_q\psi_{ab}(x_q)$ and sum: $N(c)=P^\top[(Bc)\odot(Dc)]$.
  3. Cost depends on $m$, $R'$ and $M$ only, not on the mesh. Storage is $m(2R'+M)$ numbers instead of $MR'^2$ for the precomputed tensor.
  4. No fitting: the points are a formula, the same for every mesh and dataset.

QuizCheck yourself

Why does 3-point Gauss integrate $x^5$ exactly but not $x^6$?

It is exact up to degree $2p-1=5$.

Why does equal spacing get much better when the integrand vanishes at the ends?

The periodic wrap has no seam, so the Fourier coefficients decay fast.

Why is Gauss $16^3$ useless for our 3D model?

16 points per direction cannot resolve test frequencies of 25 and above.

Why do lattices beat Gauss in 3D but not in 2D?

Gauss pays $p^d$ points; the lattice's rate does not degrade with dimension.

Why does Smolyak fail on $\psi_{12,12}$ but not on $\psi_{3,4}$?

It discards detail that is high in both directions at once.

How can an off-mesh rule be more accurate than the solver's own mesh sum?

The mesh sum carries an $O(h)$ upwind error; the off-mesh rule targets the true integral.

What still grows with the mesh in an off-mesh ROM?

Projecting the initial field and decoding the output on the mesh.

TermsGlossary

quadrature rule
points $x_q$ and weights $w_q$ approximating an integral by $\sum w_q f(x_q)$.
$m$, $p$
number of points; points per direction for a tensor rule ($m=p^d$).
geometric / algebraic
error $\sim\varrho^{-p}$ (straight line on a log plot) vs error $\sim m^{-k}$ (straight line on a log–log plot).
test function $\psi_{ab}$
the sine $\sin(a\pi x)\sin(b\pi y)$ the equation is checked against.
dual lattice / aliasing
frequencies a lattice cannot distinguish from zero; they are its entire error.
random shift
moving the whole lattice by a random offset; the spread over shifts estimates the error.
low discrepancy
points that fill every sub-box evenly (Sobol).
sparse grid
a signed combination of small tensor grids (Smolyak).
upwind difference
a one-sided derivative from the side the flow comes from; first-order accurate.
$O(h)$ gap
mesh sum minus true integral; halves when the mesh is refined.
bank $G$, $R'$, $M$
the network basis, the number of basis functions used, the number of test functions.
off-mesh
evaluating the bank at rule points that are not mesh nodes.
