Architectures we tried

Tunable NM-ROM decoder architectures, May to November 2026. Tahmid Awal, November 3, 2026.

We build reduced-order models that solve a PDE for a few numbers instead of one value per grid point. The decoder turns those few numbers into the full field, and a Gauss-Newton solver adjusts them until the field satisfies the PDE. We need two things from a decoder: the solve has to be accurate, and each solver iteration has to be cheap, or there is no point in using a reduced model.

We tried five decoder architectures. The short version:

1. ViT encoder + CP / LinearCP decoder (May–July)

u(i, j; z) = b + Σa ha(z) Xa(i) Ya(j)

Why we tried it

This was the original method. A ViT encodes a field to a latent z, a small MLP turns z into weights, and the weights multiply fixed tables of grid factors. The value at a single grid node is cheap to evaluate, so the solver only touches the few nodes picked by empirical quadrature (EQ). LinearCP adds a linear skip from z so that Poisson can start the solve from z = 0.

What happened

Why we moved on

We could not get it to work on Burgers. Better reconstruction did not help: the 2D Burgers rollout diverged even after retraining improved reconstruction 3.6×, so the Burgers results came from a frozen POD basis rather than the learned decoder. The grid factors are also tied to one mesh, and accuracy got worse as the mesh got finer. We needed a different decoder.

2. ViT decoder (May)

Why we tried it

A ViT encoder was already working, so the obvious test was a matching ViT decoder in place of CP, as a more expressive output map.

Why we moved on

It was too slow to be useful. Self-attention couples all outputs, so the solver has to run the whole decoder on all 262,144 grid nodes in every Gauss-Newton iteration, and the quadrature saves nothing. Every configuration was slower than the full solver. The solves also failed, even when the autoencoder was as good as LinearCP.

3. SIREN coordinate decoder (May)

u(x; z) = fθ(x, z)

Why we tried it

A coordinate network is not tied to a grid. It can be evaluated at any single point, so it fits the quadrature idea, and its spatial features can change with z, which CP's cannot.

What happened

Poisson 2D, N=128, against a CG full solver:

Cold-start solves also stalled in a spurious basin near z = 0 until the training was changed.

Why we moved on

It was too slow for our use. At CP-level accuracy the best SIREN was 3–8× faster than the full solver, against 183× for CP, because the whole network has to be evaluated and differentiated at every quadrature point in every iteration. The only fast version was the one where the output is linear in z and the spatial network Φ(x) is fixed, at the cost of about 3× more error. That observation is what later became the learned bank.

4. FiLM coordinate decoder (August)

u(x; z) = MLP(features(x)) with every layer scaled and shifted by functions of z

Why we tried it

FiLM represented the families much better than a plain coordinate MLP and transferred across meshes. With latent stepping (solving for z from the PDE at each time step) it became a real reduced model for Poisson, heat, Burgers and wave.

What happened

Why we moved on

Speed. Accuracy was fine, but the full network still runs and is differentiated inside every solver iteration, and cost rose steeply with latent size. The fix was to take the spatial network out of the solver loop.

5. Learned bank: our current decoder (August–now)

u(x; c) = Σa≤r ga(x) ca = Br(x) c

Why we built it

This is the fast SIREN variant taken further. A coordinate network learns a set (bank) of spatial functions ga(x). We evaluate the bank once at the quadrature points and cache it. Online, the solver only works with the cached matrix and a few coefficients, so no neural network runs inside the solver loop. At first a small nonlinear head mapped a latent z to the coefficients. Later we dropped the head and solve for the first r bank coefficients directly, because a head can never produce a field outside the bank's span. The number r is a knob we can set at deployment time to trade accuracy for cost.

Where this leaves us

The bank is the first design that is both accurate and cheap per iteration, and its cost does not grow with the mesh. On Burgers in a box it does not beat a tuned spectral solver or tuned POD, so we cannot claim a speed advantage there. What we can claim is mesh-independent cost, evaluation at any point, and lower memory. That is why the current work moved to flow past obstacles, where spectral solvers do not apply. The first obstacle bank had 18–21% velocity error; after retraining, the latest steady solves pass all seven development targets with about 0.036% velocity error. Final validation and timing are still to do, and the unsteady cylinder case (Re ≈ 100) started on November 3.
