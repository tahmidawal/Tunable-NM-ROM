Review by Reviewer 1dYR
Paper Summary

This paper proposes a reduced-order model for PDEs. It learns a spatial bank and a small
nonlinear head, then solves the projected PDE residual at query time. The bank is ordered after
training, so users can change its width or choose between the nonlinear head and the linear
span to adjust accuracy and speed.

Presentation: 3: Clear and easy to follow.

Informativeness: 2: I learned something interesting or useful.

Soundness: 2: I have minor or fixable concerns about the paper’s soundness.

Critical Strengths

The separation of the spatial bank and the head makes linear terms easy to precompute and
reduces the online cost.

Choosing different bank widths without retraining is useful. The paper also reports failed settings
and limitations clearly.

Critical Weaknesses

PCA ordering, operator precomputation and empirical quadrature are existing techniques. The
paper does not directly compare with CROM or CoLoRA, although it identifies neural-field
ROMs as its closest relatives. The gain over these methods is therefore unclear.

The neural operator comparison is not well controlled. Appendix B states that Burgers operators
use 128 training trajectories, while the proposed bank and head use 576 and 4608. The
operators also have a 3000-second training limit; the heat FNO only completes 6-7 epochs at
high resolutions (Table B.3). These differences make the accuracy comparison difficult to
interpret.

The reported speedups depend on the selected full-order solvers. Poisson and
three-dimensional heat use unpreconditioned CG comparators. Also, Table 1 compares both
model settings with a solver chosen to match the accurate setting. The fast setting therefore
needs a separate comparison at a similar error level.
Most headline results use the linear span. The nonlinear head works well on Navier-Stokes, but
its bank is a POD basis, and both bank and head are built separately for each mesh (Appendix
A.5). These results do not clearly establish the combined benefit of the nonlinear head and the
mesh-independent coordinate bank.

Questions For Authors

Could the authors compare the neural operators using the same training trajectories and allow
enough training to check whether the accuracy gap remains?

How much speedup remains against a stronger full-order solver when each model setting is
compared at a similar error level?

Could the authors compare with a neural-field ROM and separate the gains from the coordinate
bank, nonlinear head and hyper-reduction?

Reply to Reviewer 1dYR
Thank you for your review. We have added the comparisons and ablations you requested and
address the concerns and questions you raised below.
W1. Contribution and neural-field baselines
Our contribution is the bank/head factorisation: exact precomputed linear terms, a nonlinear
constraint on bank coefficients, and nested widths from one training run. PCA ordering and
quadrature are established techniques. We now compare with same-data re-implementations of
CROM and CoLoRA (Table 4; Appendix F.1). Our head has 4–5.5× lower worst-case error and
16–87× faster queries than CROM, although CROM's accurate setting has lower median error.
CoLoRA has lower worst-case error; our span has lower median error at slightly lower cost.
CoLoRA receives initial-condition parameters; ours receives a field.
W2. Matched training data and budgets
We retrained FNO and U-Net on the same 4,608 trajectories, allowing up to 8.1 hours. We
compare prediction errors here: our Burgers median error is 7–9× lower, while operators have
lower worst-case error (Table 4; Appendix F.2). This is an accuracy-only comparison with direct
prediction models; our online method solves the projected PDE. Most runs reached their time
limit before convergence, so the results are budget-limited.
W3. Stronger, accuracy-matched solvers
We now match each setting to its own error. The fast width-192 head reaches 0.35–0.48 %
worst-case error at 5.1–10.2× over the fastest equal-accuracy pseudo-spectral Navier–Stokes
solver from 450 configurations (Table 1; Appendix E.1). Errors cover three models and 128 fresh
cases; speedups use the two seeds passing timing checks. Section 5.3 and Appendix C.3 also
disclose transform-solver wins on box Poisson/heat 3D and the original Navier–Stokes family's
lack of consistent advantage after mesh coarsening.
W4. Coordinate bank and nonlinear head
Table 1 and Figure 2 use per-mesh POD banks. We separately test one coordinate-network
bank across meshes: it matches POD at 64³/96³ but trails at 128³ (Table E.1). The earlier cohort
and fallback selection after missing the preset rank-64 transfer tolerance are disclosed.
Interpolated POD transfers equally well on periodic boxes, so we remove the claim that POD
must be rebuilt per mesh.
Q1. Does the operator accuracy gap remain with matched training?
The accuracy result is mixed, as reported under W2: our median error is lower, while the
operators have lower worst-case error. Further operator training is running; we will report the
updated error comparison by tomorrow (14 November).
Q2. How much speedup survives stronger solvers?
The new Navier–Stokes result is given under W3. Table 3 and Appendix C.3 report the
corrected original comparisons, including cases where stronger solvers win.
Q3. What does each component contribute?
We added the neural-field comparisons in W1 and component ablations in Table F.3. At 16
Burgers coordinates, the head gives 2.56 % error versus 56.9 % for a linear closure and 31.8 %
for a quadratic one. Sparse nonlinear evaluation reduces time at unchanged error. Appendix F.3
separates these gains from the coordinate bank's mesh-transfer role.

Review by Reviewer iMp8
Paper Summary

The paper proposes a reduced-order model for PDEs. The solution is a sum of R basis
functions (small coordinate networks times a boundary factor) with coefficients from a small
head network. After training, the basis is reordered by PCA so that the first R′ functions give a
cheaper and less accurate model. At query time, the coefficients are found by minimizing the
PDE residual, using precomputed linear terms and empirical quadrature. Tests on Poisson,
heat, Burgers and 3D Navier–Stokes report speedups up to 27.5× (nonlinear) and over 100×
(linear).

Presentation: 3: Clear and easy to follow.

Informativeness: 1: I learned little or nothing new from this paper.

Soundness: 1: I have significant concerns about the paper’s soundness that do not appear
easy to address.

Critical Strengths

Clear cost model: space and latent code are separated, so linear terms can be precomputed.

Timing setup is described in detail, with named baselines.

Limitations are partly acknowledged.

Critical Weaknesses

Weak motivation. The test problems (Poisson, heat, Burgers on a square or cube) are cheap to
solve. Modern GPU solvers (FFT, multigrid, GPU finite elements) solve them in milliseconds to
seconds with batch (cudss) and can generate thousands of samples in seconds to minutes.
There is no point in solving these benchmarks and stressing speed nowadays.

Limited novelty. All parts are known. Neural-field ROMs, hard Dirichlet enforcement.

Mesh independence is overstated. For Navier–Stokes, the basis and head are rebuilt for each
mesh. For other problems, there is still a per-mesh setup cost (up to 869 s).

Small and weak evaluation. Test sets are small (6–64 cases), and timings have no spread.

Poor presentation. The main text has one figure (a pipeline diagram). There are no solution
plots, error maps, failure cases, or learned basis plots.

Questions For Authors

What is the total cost (data, training, setup), and how many queries are needed to break even?

How is the basis loss defined, and are coefficients solved exactly at each step?

Can it handle other boundary conditions and complex geometry without a hand-made boundary
factor?

Please compare with FFT/multigrid GPU solvers and show solution visualizations.

Reply to Reviewer iMp8
Thank you for your review. We have strengthened the solver comparisons, clarified the scope
and cost of the method, and added the requested visual evidence.
W1. Motivation and comparisons with modern solvers
We disagree that fast GPU solvers remove the motivation for reduced models. Our target is
repeated PDE queries after offline training and assembly, with query-time accuracy–cost control.
We compare against accuracy-matched GPU solvers and distinguish reduced-solve cost from
full-query cost.
Our Navier–Stokes head is 3.3–11.7× faster than the fastest accuracy-matched solver from a
450-entry GPU pseudo-spectral ladder; the fast width-192 setting gives 5.1–10.2× (Table 1;
Appendix E.1). Incompressibility already uses modewise projection, so there is no sparse
pressure system for multigrid or cuDSS to accelerate. Batching both codes gives 17× at 128³
and 75× at 64³ for batch 128, including our input/output work.
Figure G.3 demonstrates the scaling advantage on a GPU: increasing the Burgers 2D resolution
from 256² to 4096², a 256-fold increase in grid points, leaves reduced-solve time essentially
unchanged at fixed bank width and solver settings on one H200. Input projection and full-field
output remain mesh-dependent; our flat-cost claim concerns the reduced solve.
We retain the measured limits: transforms win on box Poisson/3D heat, and input/output cost
removes a consistent full-query advantage at 256³. We are running GPU cuDSS and AmgX
single-solve benchmarks and will report the results as soon as they are available, separately
from complete PDE-query comparisons.
W2. Contribution
The contribution is a physics-based reduced model with a query-time accuracy–cost ladder from
one training run. Bank/head factorisation makes linear terms exact precomputed matrices and
constrains online dynamics within the bank span. Figure 2 and Table F.3 isolate the head's
benefit; Table 4 compares neural-field baselines. Sections 3 and 5.5 distinguish the contribution
from established PCA ordering and quadrature.
W3. Mesh independence and setup
Mesh independence concerns the learned representation and reduced online computation.
Per-mesh operator assembly is an explicit offline cost. Table E.1 demonstrates one coordinate
bank across meshes, including unchanged-head reuse. Table 1 uses per-mesh POD and is
labelled accordingly. Interpolated POD also transfers on periodic boxes; our claim does not
require rebuilding POD on every mesh.
W4. Evaluation size and timing variability
Our main revised evaluation uses 128 fresh cases and three independently trained models
(Table 1). Appendix C.4 reports GPU timing spread. Speedups use the two seeds passing
timing checks. The third seed's accuracy remains included, with its failed timing gate disclosed
in Appendix E.1. Exploratory cohorts and failed retrains are labelled.
W5. Solution plots and presentation
We added a main-text head-versus-span comparison (Figure 2). Appendix G supplies solution
fields, error maps, learned bases, failure cases, mesh scaling and break-even curves.
Q1. Total cost and break-even
We target repeated queries within one parameterised family. Appendix C.2, Table C.4 reports
offline costs and break-even counts: about 43,000 queries for Navier–Stokes at 128³. Missing
data-generation costs are identified. Against transforms, box Poisson never breaks even, and
heat 2D needs about 370,000 queries.
Q2. Basis loss and exact coefficient solves
We give the losses and weights in Appendix C.1, Eqs. (20)–(23) and Table C.3. Only heat 3D
and Burgers 3D eliminate coefficients at every training step through thin QR. Heat 3D uses a
weighted POD-mode mean and a snapshot-minibatch tail term. Other banks jointly train
coefficients or latent codes, with the stated least-squares resets/phases. After freezing the bank,
ordering coefficients and head targets are exact least-squares fits; online coefficients come from
the projected PDE solve.
Q3. Geometry and boundary conditions
We demonstrate cylinder flow using smooth boundary-distance factors and an inflow lift in a
Galerkin rollout (Appendix E.2). Automatic handling of arbitrary geometry and Neumann/Robin
conditions remains untested.
Q4. FFT/multigrid comparisons and visualisations

See W1 for the GPU numerical-solver comparisons and W5 for visualisations. We are running
GPU cuDSS and AmgX single-solve benchmarks and will report the results as soon as they are
available.

Review by Reviewer 8ZEF
Paper Summary
This paper proposes a runtime-tunable reduced-order modeling framework for efficient PDE
solving. It combines a learned coordinate-based spatial bank with linear-span and
nonlinear-manifold representations, using matrix-free Petrov–Galerkin projection for efficient
online solving. The ordered spatial bank allows users to adjust the accuracy–efficiency trade-off
without retraining. Experiments on Poisson, heat, Burgers, and Navier–Stokes equations
demonstrate promising accuracy and speedups over full-order solvers and existing
learning-based approaches.

Presentation: 2: Generally understandable, but requires substantial effort in places.

Informativeness: 2: I learned something interesting or useful.

Soundness: 3: I have no substantive concerns about the paper’s soundness.

Critical Strengths

Practical and flexible design. The ability to switch between linear-span and nonlinear-manifold
representations and adjust the basis width at inference time is useful. The integration of
coordinate-based spatial banks, precomputation, and hyper-reduction makes the framework
computationally efficient.

Strong empirical results. The experiments cover multiple PDE families and resolutions,
demonstrating substantial speedups and encouraging cross-resolution performance. The
comparisons with both conventional ROMs and neural operators provide useful evidence of the
method's practical value.

Critical Weaknesses

Limited diversity of nonlinear evaluations. Although the paper includes Burgers and
Navier–Stokes equations, the most accurate configurations for several tasks use the linear-span
representation. Additional challenging nonlinear PDE benchmarks would help establish when
the nonlinear-manifold component provides substantial benefits and how broadly the framework
generalizes.

Some limitations in baseline comparisons. The reported speedups depend on the selected
full-order solvers, and the neural operator comparisons use different training data sizes and
computational budgets in some settings. More closely matched comparisons would strengthen
the empirical conclusions.

Questions For Authors

Could the authors provide additional experiments on more challenging nonlinear PDEs,
particularly problems with stronger nonlinear dynamics, to further demonstrate the benefits of
the nonlinear-manifold representation?
Could the authors further clarify when the nonlinear head is preferable to the linear span, and
whether its advantages extend beyond the current Navier–Stokes setting?

How might the reported speedups change when compared with more optimized full-order
solvers or neural operators trained under more closely matched budgets?

Minor Suggestion: The paper is generally well organized and easy to follow. However, Figure 1
could be more visually polished, with a cleaner layout, more consistent typography, and clearer
separation between offline training and online inference. This would further improve the
presentation.

Dear Area Chair,
We would like to distinguish the broad motivation question raised in reviewer iMp8's W1 from
the paper-specific methodological and empirical assessment. The availability of fast full-order
solvers is a reason to require strong comparisons; it does not by itself establish that reduced
models have no useful role.
Our contribution is a learned low-dimensional representation with query-time accuracy–cost
choices, while retaining a projected PDE solve for each query. The intended use is repeated
simulation within a parameterised family. The revised manuscript addresses the specific
baseline and evaluation concerns through accuracy-matched numerical solvers, matched-data
operator error comparisons, fresh test cases and independently trained models.
In particular, Table 1 provides measured advantages over the tested accuracy-matched GPU
solver on the stated Navier–Stokes family. We also report transform-solver wins, offline
amortisation costs and the limits of mesh-independent reduced computation. We respectfully
ask that the assessment distinguish the general motivation question from this narrower
contribution and revised evidence.
