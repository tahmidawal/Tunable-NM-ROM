\section{Related work and what is new here}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Linear and nonlinear manifold ROMs.}
Projection-based reduction on a linear subspace \citep{Sirovich1987POD,
BennerGugercinWillcox2015} is limited on advection-dominated and
moving-front problems by the slow decay of the Kolmogorov width
\citep{CohenDeVore2015,OhlbergerRave2016}. Nonlinear manifold ROMs replace the
subspace with the image of a learned decoder: \citet{LeeCarlberg2020} introduced
convolutional-autoencoder manifolds with least-squares Petrov--Galerkin
projection \citep{Carlberg2011LSPG}, and \citet{KimChoi2022NMROM} made the
decoder evaluable on a sample mesh with a shallow masked autoencoder, which is
what makes hyper-reduction possible at all on a nonlinear manifold. Our trial
manifold \eqref{eq:decoder} is of exactly this family. The decoder we use is a
separable one --- a coordinate network supplying a spatial bank, composed with a
nonlinear coefficient map --- rather than a convolutional or masked-dense
decoder, and it is this factorisation, not the projection, that we rely on. The
projection itself is standard; we claim no novelty for it, and we state the
implemented test space, row scaling and stopping rule explicitly
(\S\ref{sec:method:weak}) because conflating tangent Galerkin with residual
least squares was a defect of our own earlier presentation.

\paragraph{Precomputed reduced operators.}
Caching reduced tensors for polynomial operators is established. Tensorial POD
\citep{StefanescuSandu2014} precomputes the reduced coefficients of polynomial
nonlinearities so that the online residual and Jacobian are contractions of
fixed arrays, and compressive tensor approximations appear already in
\citet{Carlberg2011LSPG}. Quadratic manifolds
\citep{GeelenWrightWillcox2022,BarnettFarhat2022} make the decoder itself
polynomial, which yields online-efficient reduced models by the same mechanism;
\citet{WederSchwerdtnerPeherstorfer2024} give online-efficient Neural Galerkin
models on quadratic manifolds for linear PDEs. Relative to this line,
\emph{the tensor construction in \eqref{eq:tensor} is not new}. What differs is
the pairing: the coefficient map $\head$ is an unrestricted neural network while
only the \emph{discrete field operator} is required to be polynomial, so the
manifold is not itself quadratic and the guarantees available for quadratic
manifolds do not transfer. Whether the extra coefficient nonlinearity is worth
its cost at matched reduced dimension is an empirical question, and we treat it
as one (\S\ref{sec:exp:head}); exact tensor algebra by itself establishes
nothing about that. We also note explicitly that the canonical-polyadic decoder
of our previous submission admits the same preassembly, so precomputability is
not a property of the newer bank: for that architecture one may write
$\jac{\umanifold}\T(\Aop u-f)=\dhead\T[\,C\head-d\,]$ with $C=\bank\T\Aop\bank$
and $d=\bank\T(f-\Aop\lift)$. This is an algebraic observation about the printed
architecture, not a measured statement about its implementation.

\paragraph{Hyper-reduction.}
Where the projected operator is not exactly precomputable we use empirical
quadrature in the non-negative least-squares form of \citet{Hernandez2017EQ},
with the linear-programming variant of \citet{YanoPatera2019LPEQ} and energy-based
mesh sampling \citep{FarhatECSW2014} as the nearby alternatives, and DEIM
\citep{ChaturantabutSorensen2010} as the interpolation-based one. We use this
machinery unchanged. Two things we do differently are worth stating, both of them
corrections rather than contributions: the quadrature fits only the term that
resists preassembly, since the linear terms are exact by
\eqref{eq:linear-precomp} and need no sampling at all; and the node count is a
selection among rules fitted offline, not a continuous online control
(\S\ref{sec:method:eq}). The comparison we care about is therefore between exact
preassembly and a fitted rule on the same frozen decoder, holding tests, row
scaling, initialisation, time discretisation, stopping and output contract fixed
--- including the offline cost of each.

\paragraph{Neural operators.}
FNO \citep{Li2020FNO}, DeepONet \citep{Lu2021DeepONet} and the broader
operator-learning literature \citep{Kovachki2023NeuralOperator} learn a map
between function spaces and evaluate it in a single forward pass. They are
non-intrusive: they need no discrete operator at inference and apply where no
solver exists, which is a capability our method does not have. They also
produce one accuracy--cost point per trained model, which is the asymmetry our
solver-side controls address (\S\ref{sec:method:tuning}). We do not treat these
as substitutes for one another, and we do not present a head-to-head table as a
verdict on either family: we state the condition under which the comparison is
meaningful (\S\ref{sec:method:intrusive}), hold the training data and split
fixed, and report the result whichever way it falls. In the screens completed so
far the neural operator is the more accurate model on the elliptic problem
(\gen{Poisson matched-data screen: per-capacity worst and mean error, ROM and
FNO}), and we report that rather than omitting it.

\paragraph{Classical solvers.}
The separable, constant-coefficient cells admit a direct discrete sine-transform
solve \citep{Swarztrauber1977}, which is faster than every reduced model we have
measured on those cells; preconditioned Krylov methods \citep{Saad2003} are the
appropriate iterative comparator, and a reduced model that beats one iterative
solver has not thereby beaten the others. We name the direct solver wherever it
applies and report it alongside, rather than choosing the comparator that
flatters the method. The question a reduced model can be asked on these problems
is not whether it beats a fast direct solver on a rectangle --- it does not ---
but what happens where no such solver exists.

\paragraph{What we claim, and what we do not.}
The contribution we are prepared to defend is a combination and its
characterisation: a frozen separable decoder whose spatial bank permits both
exact preassembly of the linear and polynomial parts of a weak discrete
residual and sampled evaluation of the parts that resist it; a precisely stated
weak least-squares solve with recorded stationarity and exit reasons; and
measurements of when the compression pays and when it does not, including the
cases where it does not. We do not claim a new projection, a new hyper-reduction
algorithm, a new tensor construction, a speedup at matched accuracy against a
tuned classical solver, dominance over neural operators, or an accuracy--cost
frontier. Where the evidence is a single training seed on development cases, we
say so next to the number.
