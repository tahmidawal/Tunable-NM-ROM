\begin{abstract}

Neural operators such as Fourier Neural Operators \citep{Li2020FNO} and
DeepONets \citep{Lu2021DeepONet} deliver one (accuracy, speed) point per
trained model; at deployment only the evaluation grid can be changed,
which moves cost but not what the model can represent. We present a non-linear manifold reduced order
model (NM-ROM) for elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic PDEs that exposes a
family of accuracy/cost operating points from a single trained
decoder, controlled at inference time by one primary knob, the rank $q$
of a linear correction the solver may switch on, and three solver-side
knobs: the iteration cap, the stopping tolerance, and the
empirical-quadrature (EQ) sample count. The framework combines a matrix-free least-squares Petrov--Galerkin
projection of the discrete PDE residual onto fixed smooth weak tests,
evaluated through JAX autodiff \citep{Bradbury2018JAX}; NNLS-based
\citep{LawsonHanson1974NNLS} EQ hyper-reduction
\citep{Hernandez2017EQ,YanoPatera2019LPEQ} validated on the states the
solver actually reaches; exact boundary condition enforcement inside the
decoder; and a decoder --- a per-node spatial bank from a Fourier-feature
coordinate network \citep{Tancik2020FourierFeatures} times a small neural
head with a linear skip, plus nested correction directions --- identified
against three constraints: cold-start convergence, EQ compatibility, and
mesh-independent per-node evaluation. Across 2D Burgers, Poisson, heat
and waves, the trained-once family is monotone in $q$ on Burgers on each
of three training seeds and, with the test count held fixed so that $q$
is the only control, meets a bar fixed before any run; its quadrature
rules are validated on reachable states, not by their fitting residual;
and its wins are cost-only and measured in the same job: $\nLshapeNeuralCheaper\times$ and
$\nLshapeNeuralCheaperFiveTwelve\times$ cheaper than a sparse direct solve
on an L-shaped domain at $256^2$ and $512^2$, and non-dominated on the
evolved-times metric at $1024^2$ on Burgers. Nothing reduced is on the
frontier at $256^2$, where a tuned full-order solver and a neural operator
trained on the same data are both cheaper and more accurate; and on
Poisson, heat and waves the family collapses to a linear model, which
says when the nonlinear manifold is worth having.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Neural operators for partial differential equations (PDEs)
\citep{Li2020FNO,Lu2021DeepONet,Kovachki2023NeuralOperator,Li2024PINO,BoulleTownsend2024} have
become a strong empirical baseline, but each trained model produces
essentially one (accuracy, wall-clock) operating point: the only
deployment-time knob, the evaluation grid, moves cost without changing
what the model can represent, and it worked for one of the four operator
arms we tested (\S\ref{sec:results:operators}). Classical Full Order Methods (FOMs)
\citep{Hughes2000FEM} solved iteratively
\citep{HestenesStiefel1952CG} expose this knob through their iteration
tolerance, and where a fast transform applies they are hard to beat on
wall-clock at the resolutions of interest.

Two families of methods sit on either side of this gap. Neural
operators and PDE foundation models
\citep{HerdePoseidon2024,McCabeMPP2024,HaoDPOT2024,Chen2024UnsupervisedNO} deliver fast amortised inference but fix what the model can represent at
training time, so a materially different accuracy needs retraining. Reduced order
models (ROMs) address the same problem from the opposite direction: linear
projection-based ROMs \citep{BennerGugercinWillcox2015,Sirovich1987POD}
inherit the FOM's guarantees but hit the Kolmogorov $n$-width barrier
on advection-dominated regimes \citep{CohenDeVore2015}, and non-linear
manifold ROMs (NM-ROMs) \citep{LeeCarlberg2020,KimChoi2022NMROM} lift
this barrier with a learned manifold but have so far been demonstrated
mainly on 1D advection-dominated benchmarks rather than head-to-head
against neural operators and tuned full-order solvers in the same job.
The key open question is thus whether a single trained NM-ROM can expose
a deployment-time accuracy/cost tradeoff that neither alternative
offers, and where, if anywhere, that tradeoff is worth having once the
comparators are measured in the same allocation.

This paper answers that question with measurements on 2D Burgers,
Poisson, heat and waves, and the answer is mixed. We present an NM-ROM
framework whose distinguishing property is a \emph{deployment-time
accuracy/cost family traced by a single trained model}: at inference,
one primary knob --- the rank $q$ of a linear correction the solver may
switch on --- and three solver-side knobs --- the iteration cap, the
stopping tolerance, and the Empirical Quadrature sample count ---
trade accuracy for cost. The supporting machinery is a decoder that enforces Dirichlet boundary
conditions exactly, a damped Levenberg--Marquardt latent solver
\citep{Marquardt1963,NocedalWright2006}, NNLS-based Empirical Quadrature
\citep{Hernandez2017EQ,YanoPatera2019LPEQ} validated on reachable
states, and a matrix-free JAX implementation \citep{Bradbury2018JAX}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A tunable NM-ROM with a deployment-time accuracy/cost
      family from a single trained model, and a matched-dimension
      result.} The correction rank $q$ moves accuracy and the solver-side
      knobs move cost. With the test count $M$ held fixed so that $q$ is
      the only control, the Burgers $256^2$ ladder is monotone with every
      rung converged, spanning $\nQxmErrSpan\times$ in error for
      $\nQxmCostSpan\times$ in cost inside one allocation and meeting a
      bar fixed before any run (\S\ref{sec:results:tunability}); at
      matched $k=16$ inside one bank the neural head reaches
      $\nAblBurgersNeural\,\%$ where the best linear map reaches
      $\nAblBurgersLinear\,\%$ and POD-16 $\nAblBurgersPodSixteen\,\%$
      (\S\ref{sec:results:head}).
\item \textbf{Architectural choices that make this family
      achievable.} We identify the three constraints a decoder must
      satisfy --- cold-start convergence, EQ compatibility, and
      mesh-independent per-node evaluation --- and show that a separable
      decoder (coordinate-network bank, head with a linear skip, nested
      corrections) meets all three.
\item \textbf{Precomputed weak operators and validated quadrature that
      make the framework practical.} Exact preassembly of every linear
      term, Empirical Quadrature for the one nonlinear term, and a rule
      that a fitted quadrature is accepted only by its held-out error on
      reachable states and by independent re-draws, never by its fitting
      residual.
\item \textbf{Where the family is not worth having, reported as such.}
      At $256^2$ on the square no reduced subject is on the
      non-dominated set once a tuned full-order solver and a neural
      operator on the same data are in the job; on Poisson, heat and
      waves the top rung is the linear model and also the cheapest
      point. The wins are cost-only: $\nLshapeNeuralCheaper\times$ and
      $\nLshapeNeuralCheaperFiveTwelve\times$ cheaper than a sparse direct
      solve on an L-shaped domain at $256^2$ and $512^2$, and
      non-dominated at $1024^2$ on Burgers on the evolved-times metric
      only (\S\ref{sec:results:operators}, \S\ref{sec:results:collapse}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What we do not claim.} No speedup over an efficient
full-order solver on the square at $256^2$; no accuracy superiority over
neural operators; no frontier over POD at every rank; no cost ratio
across jobs or GPUs; no convergence theory; and every number is
development-cohort evidence, single-seed except for the Burgers
scheduled ladder (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Linear and non-linear manifold ROMs.}
Linear projection-based ROMs
\citep{BennerGugercinWillcox2015,Sirovich1987POD,Berkooz1993,QuarteroniManzoniNegri2015,HesthavenRozzaStamm2016}
suffer from the Kolmogorov $n$-width barrier on advection-dominated and
moving-front problems
\citep{CohenDeVore2015,GreifUrban2019,OhlbergerRave2016}. NM-ROMs lift
this barrier by using a non-linear trial manifold:
\citet{LeeCarlberg2020} introduced convolutional-autoencoder NM-ROMs
with LSPG projection \citep{Carlberg2011LSPG}, and subsequent work has
explored shallow masked autoencoders with sample-mesh hyper-reduction
\citep{KimChoi2022NMROM}, reduced over-collocation
\citep{RomorStabileRozza2023}, domain-decomposed sparse autoencoders
\citep{Diaz2024DomainDecompNMROM}, quadratic manifolds with analytic
Jacobian regularity \citep{GeelenWrightWillcox2022,BarnettFarhat2022},
and implicit-feature-tracking variants with EQ
\citep{MirhoseiniZahr2023}. Related lines include POD-pre-compressed
DL-ROMs \citep{FrescaManzoni2022PODDLROM} and conditional-neural-field
ROMs with approximate-distance BC enforcement
\citep{KimWenLeeChoiCNFROM2024}. We differ from these by a separable
decoder --- a coordinate-network spatial bank
\citep{Tancik2020FourierFeatures} times a small neural head with a linear
skip --- augmented by nested \emph{correction} directions chosen offline
once, so that a rank $q$ is selected at run time without any refit, and
by exposing $q$ with three solver-side knobs as a monotone accuracy/cost
family from one trained decoder, analogous to runtime-tunable networks
\citep{YuSlimmable2019,Cai2020OnceForAll}. NN-augmented projection ROMs
\citep{BarnettFarhatMaday2023} and online-adaptive bases
\citep{PeherstorferWillcox2015Adaptive,Carlberg2015hRefinement} add a
nonlinear correction to a linear basis; ours is the reverse composition,
the nonlinear head inside the linear bank with the linear part as the
correction. The projection is least-squares Petrov--Galerkin
\citep{Carlberg2011LSPG} and the linear terms are preassembled as in
tensorial POD \citep{StefanescuSandu2014,WederSchwerdtnerPeherstorfer2024};
we claim no novelty for either.

\paragraph{Neural operators, hyper-reduction, and differentiable programming.}
Neural-operator surrogates
\citep{Li2020FNO,Lu2021DeepONet,Kovachki2023NeuralOperator,LiGeoFNO2023,TranFFNO2023,Cao2021GalerkinTransformer,LiOFormer2023,HaoGNOT2023,WuTransolver2024,WangLNO2024,WenGAOT2025,HuLinearNO2025,Shikhman2026BoundaryIndexed}
and PDE foundation models
\citep{HerdePoseidon2024,McCabeMPP2024,HaoDPOT2024,HolzschuhPDETransformer2025}
deliver one fixed (accuracy, speed) point per trained model and
typically approximate boundary conditions via penalty terms; partial
fixes via distance functions
\citep{SukumarSrivastava2022,BerroneBC2022}, hard-constrained
DeepONets \citep{BrechtHardConstraints2023}, BC-embedded operators
\citep{WangBENO2024}, and diffusion-style refinement
\citep{LippePDERefiner2023} address pieces of these limitations in
isolation. Hybrid solver-in-the-loop methods
\citep{UmSolverInTheLoop2020,Kochkov2021PNAS,KopanicakovaKarniadakis2024,EshaghiNOWS2025,Raissi2019,Karniadakis2021PIML}
embed operators in classical iterations. We instead use the network
solely for manifold learning and rely on a least-squares Petrov--Galerkin
projection \citep{Carlberg2011LSPG} for the PDE residual, and we compare
against an FNO, a U-Net \citep{Ronneberger2015UNet}, the standard strong
baseline of PDEBench \citep{Takamoto2022PDEBench}, and a Transolver
\citep{WuTransolver2024} on identical data at an equal wall budget
(\S\ref{sec:results:operators}). For hyper-reduction we retain classical
NNLS-based EQ \citep{Hernandez2017EQ,YanoPatera2019LPEQ,LawsonHanson1974NNLS}
over DEIM \citep{ChaturantabutSorensen2010}, ECSW
\citep{FarhatECSW2014,Chapman2017AcceleratedECSW}, GNAT
\citep{Carlberg2013GNAT}, matrix-DEIM \citep{NegriManzoniAmsallem2015},
neural-DEIM \citep{HirschPichiHesthavenNEIM2024}, and high-order EIM
\citep{Nguyen2024HighOrderEIM}; the difference is that only the
nonlinear advection term is sampled and that a fitted rule is accepted
by its held-out error and by re-draws, not by its fit residual, with the
projected system evaluated matrix-free through JAX
\citep{Bradbury2018JAX,GriewankWalther2008}. On separable
constant-coefficient cells the discrete sine transform is a direct solve
\citep{Swarztrauber1977} faster than every reduced model we measure, and
preconditioned Krylov methods \citep{HestenesStiefel1952CG,Saad2003} are
the iterative comparator; we name the direct solver wherever it applies.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

We construct an NM-ROM solver by turning a learned manifold into a
practical PDE solver through three components: a non-linear trial
manifold whose decoder enforces Dirichlet boundary conditions exactly and
carries nested linear correction directions
(\S\ref{sec:method:manifold}); a least-squares Petrov--Galerkin
projection of the discrete residual onto fixed smooth weak tests
(\S\ref{sec:method:weak}); and exact preassembly of every linear term
with Empirical Quadrature for the one nonlinear term
(\S\ref{sec:method:precomp}); the decoder architecture (\S\ref{sec:decoder})
supports these requirements. Throughout, $u \in \Real^{n}$ is the
full-order state on a uniform grid of $N$ intervals per axis,
$\Aop \in \Real^{n \times n}$ the negative five-point Laplacian on
$[0,1]^2$; $R$ is the bank width, $k$ the latent dimension, $M$ the number
of weak tests and $m$ the number of quadrature nodes.
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} (Appendix~\ref{app:arch}) shows the data flow;
Appendix~\ref{app:method} gives the per-PDE derivations and exit codes.

\subsection{Trial Manifold with Exact Dirichlet Enforcement}
\label{sec:method:manifold}

We train a decoder $\mathcal{D} : \Real^k \to \Real^n$, $k \ll n$, with
latent state $z$; there is no encoder in the deployed path. The decoder
is separable: a frozen spatial \emph{bank} $\bank\in\Real^{n\times R}$
times a small neural \emph{head} $\head:\Real^{k}\to\Real^{R}$,
$k\le R\ll n$, with the bank built once per mesh from a
random-Fourier-feature coordinate network $g_\phi$
\citep{Tancik2020FourierFeatures},
\begin{equation}
  \bank_{x,:} = \bcmask(x)\,g_\phi(x)\T,
  \qquad
  \bcmask(x) = 16\,x_1(1-x_1)\,x_2(1-x_2).
  \label{eq:bank}
\end{equation}
To strictly enforce the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on the
boundary set $\Gamma$, we utilize the smooth vanishing factor $\bcmask$,
zero on $\Gamma$, folded into every column of the bank. This ensures
$\partial u/\partial z = 0$ on $\Gamma$ at every resolution, naturally
annihilating boundary equations during the projection; as implemented it
covers homogeneous data only.

\paragraph{The correction ladder.}
\label{sec:method:ladder}
The deployed model augments the head's output with $q$ fixed directions
$\corr\in\Real^{R\times q}$ and solves for the latent code and the
correction coefficients together,
\begin{equation}
  u(\latent,y) \;=\; \bank\big(\head(\latent) + \corr\,y\big),
  \qquad \latent\in\Real^{k},\; y\in\Real^{q},\; 0\le q\le R .
  \label{eq:ladder}
\end{equation}
The columns of $\corr$ are chosen offline once per checkpoint by a
principal component analysis of the head's own residual on the training
codes, ordered so that $C_{q}$ is a prefix of $C_{q'}$ for $q<q'$; the
ladder is nested and no rung needs retraining. At $q=0$ the model is the
frozen head; at $q=R$ the head is irrelevant and the model is the linear
reduced model on the bank's span, solved without a nonlinear iteration;
in between, $q$ moves the reachable set from the head's image towards
the bank's span, and the solved dimension from $k$ to $k+q$. Two
consequences organise everything that follows: the bank's projection
error is a floor on the achievable error whatever the head or the solver
does, so nonlinearity in $\head$ buys a smaller \emph{solved} dimension,
not an escape from the span; and all $x$-dependence factors through
$\bank$, so the decoder restricted to any node set is a cached block
times the coefficients, which makes node sampling and exact preassembly
available from one decoder.

\subsection{Projecting the PDE onto the Manifold}
\label{sec:method:weak}

Each PDE we consider gives a discretised residual $\rfull(u)$ that
should vanish at the true solution. We substitute the trial manifold
$u(\latent,y)$ into $\rfull$ and project onto a fixed, latent-independent
test space: $\tests\in\Real^{M\times n}$ collects the $M$ lowest
tensor-product sine vectors of the square, eigenvectors of the five-point
operator, $\tests\Aop=\rowscale\tests$. The reduced problem solved online
is
\begin{equation}
  (\latent^{\star},y^{\star})
  =
  \argmin_{\latent,\,y}\;
  \tfrac12\,\norm{\rweak(\latent,y)}_2^2,
  \qquad
  \rweak(\latent,y) = \rowscale_\star^{-1}\,\tests\,\rfull\!\big(u(\latent,y)\big)
  \in\Real^{M},
  \qquad M>k+q ,
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
with a diagonal row scaling $\rowscale_\star$ stated per PDE in
Appendix~\ref{app:method}. This is a least-squares Petrov--Galerkin
condition with an explicit test space
\citep{Carlberg2011LSPG,LeeCarlberg2020}, not tangent Galerkin, and
overdetermined rather than square; it yields a $(k+q)$-dimensional system
that inherits the structure of the original PDE and that we solve by a
method appropriate to that structure.

\paragraph{Elliptic (Poisson).}
The Poisson residual is $\rfull(u) = \Aop u - f$. Projection gives
$\rweak(\latent,y)=B_0(\head(\latent)+\corr y)-b_0$ with
$B_0=\tests\bank$ (Appendix~\ref{app:method:poisson}). Because the
projector onto $\operatorname{range}(B_0\corr)$ does not depend on
$\latent$, $y$ is eliminated exactly \citep{GolubPereyra1973} and the
nonlinear iteration stays $k$-dimensional at every $q$; at $q=R$ the
model is a linear least-squares solve with no iteration at all.

\paragraph{Parabolic (heat).}
The heat semi-discretisation $du/dt = -\kappa\, \Aop u$ is advanced with
Crank--Nicolson; the reduced step substitutes the manifold into the fully
discrete equation \emph{before} projecting and solves
$\latent_{n+1}=\argmin_{\latent}\norm{B_0\head(\latent)-D\,B_0\head(\latent_n)}_2$,
$D$ the diagonal Crank--Nicolson amplification of the retained modes
(Appendix~\ref{app:method:heat}): a nonlinear least-squares problem, not a
linear system in $\latent_{n+1}$. At $q=R$ the trajectory is the exact
modal propagation of the bank coefficients, with no head and no
iteration.

\paragraph{Hyperbolic (Burgers).}
For $u_t+u(u_x+u_y)=\nu\Delta u$ with a sign-upwind stencil and backward
Euler (Appendix~\ref{app:method:burgers}) the weak residual is quadratic
in the coefficients, so $y$ is not eliminated in closed form; we damp the
$(\latent,y)$ blocks separately inside one Levenberg--Marquardt step
(\emph{block-damped} variable projection), which exits stationary
everywhere where a joint LM leaves $\nBlockJointBudgetExits$ budget exits
(Table~\ref{tab:solver-variants}).

\paragraph{Solver and exits.}
Each attempt solves the damped normal system
$(H+\lambda\,\diag(\diag H))\,\delta=-g$, $H=\jac{}\T\jac{}$,
$g=\jac{}\T\rweak$, from forward-mode differentiation through $\head$
\citep{Bradbury2018JAX}, accepting the step only if it strictly decreases
the residual: damped Levenberg--Marquardt with a monotone test
\citep{Marquardt1963}. Three exit families are recorded and never
conflated: the scale-free stationarity measure
\begin{equation}
  \station{\latent}{y}=\norm{\jac{}\T\rweak}_2\,/\,\big(\normF{\jac{}}\,\norm{\rweak}_2\big)\le\eta_{\mathrm{tol}},
  \label{eq:stationarity}
\end{equation}
a residual threshold, and the stalls, whose outputs are reported as
early-stopped, never as converged. No query uses the solution it
predicts: the elliptic solve starts from the cached training code nearest
the projected source, and the Burgers query fits $(\latent,y)$ to the
supplied initial field on a fixed rule independent of the query mesh
(Appendix~\ref{app:method:codes}).

\subsection{Hyper-reduction}
\label{sec:method:precomp}

The non-linear manifold reduces the DOF count from $n$ to $k+q$, but a
projected term such as $\tests N(\bank c)$ still costs $O(n)$ to
evaluate, which kills the wall-clock benefit. For a fixed linear operator
the tested residual is exactly precomputable:
$\tests(\Aop\,u-f)=B\,(\head(\latent)+\corr y)-b$ with
$B=\tests\Aop\bank=\rowscale\,\tests\bank\in\Real^{M\times R}$ assembled
offline by two one-dimensional sine transforms, so linear terms are never
approximated, on any PDE here. For Burgers the advection $\tests N(\bank c)$
is the only term that resists preassembly; it is evaluated either
\emph{densely} on every interior node, $O(nR)$ per residual, or on an
\emph{empirical quadrature} rule of $m$ nodes with non-negative weights
\citep{Hernandez2017EQ,YanoPatera2019LPEQ}, $O(mR)$ through the cached
stencil block. The rule is a non-negative \emph{per-node} weight vector, \emph{independent
of the snapshot}, fitted by NNLS \citep{LawsonHanson1974NNLS} to reproduce
the projected advection term at $n_{\rm fit}$ stored codes with a hard cap
of $m$ nodes (Appendix~\ref{app:method:eq}); changing $m$ re-solves the
fit, rules are not nested, and deployment selects among stored rules. \emph{A rule is never accepted on its NNLS fit residual}; we score it by
the held-out relative error $\rho$ of the projected advection term
\eqref{eq:rho} over states the solver actually reaches on trajectories
disjoint from the fit and evaluation cases, against a primary bar
$\rho_{\max}\le\nEqtopBar$ fixed before any certification job ran and a
tight bar $\nEqtopTightBar$, and then re-draw the construction: a rule is
\emph{confirmed} only if every re-draw passes
(\S\ref{sec:results:tunability}).

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:decoder}

Three properties of the problem motivate our architecture. First, the
latent iteration is initialised cold, so the decoder Jacobian must retain
a well-conditioned linear component for the first step to descend into
the manifold; this motivates a \emph{linear skip} in the head. Second,
hyper-reduction retains a sparse subset of mesh nodes, so the decoder
must evaluate at any single node in mesh-independent time; this motivates
a \emph{per-node bank} from a coordinate network. Third, the family must
reach from the head's image to the bank's whole span without retraining;
this motivates the nested \emph{correction directions}.

\paragraph{Linear skip, for cold-start convergence.}
The head is a two-layer SiLU MLP plus a linear skip,
$\head(\latent)=\varphi_\theta(\latent)+W\T\latent$, so its Jacobian
$\dhead=D\varphi_\theta+W\T$ retains a latent-independent component
along which the cold solve can descend; the elliptic solver checks the
numerical rank of the projected Jacobian at every query. The skip is a
design choice; we do not ablate it in this paper.

\paragraph{No encoder; per-node bank.}
Elliptic solutions are globally coupled through the Green's function,
which is why the previous design carried a global encoder; none is
needed, since the query fits $(\latent,y)$ against the supplied input.
Empirical Quadrature only saves wall-clock time if the decoder can be
evaluated at one node without touching the rest of the grid: the decoder
restricted to the rule's support is a cached block times the
coefficients, and the network's parameters do not depend on the mesh.

\paragraph{What is fixed, what is chosen, and how error is reported.}
\label{sec:method:tuning}
Every operating point uses one frozen artefact per PDE ($\theta$,
$\bank$, $R$, $k$, $\corr$, the time discretisation and the query
contract); selected at run time are the rank $q$, the quadrature (dense
or a stored validated rule) and the stopping tolerance and budget; $M$ is
held fixed along the headline ladder.
\label{sec:method:layers}
Every panel reports three numbers: the \emph{bank floor} (the projection
error onto $\operatorname{range}\bank$, what no head and no solver can
beat), the \emph{best-found} error (the smallest any point of the
augmented manifold attains, from a multistart oracle) and the
\emph{solved} error the iteration returns; a lever can only move the
layer it acts on.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

\subsection{Matrix-free Projected Operators via JAX}

To overcome the bottleneck of explicitly forming the Jacobian of the
decoder, the framework is implemented utilizing hardware-accelerated
automatic differentiation. Every linear term is preassembled once per
mesh as the $M\times R$ matrix $B$, so the online residual and Jacobian
for a linear PDE are $B\,(\head(\latent)+\corr y)-b$ and
$B\,[\dhead\;\;\corr]$, with $\dhead$ from a forward-mode Jacobian-Vector
Product through the head \citep{Bradbury2018JAX}; for Burgers the sampled
advection term is evaluated on the cached stencil block of the rule's
support. The damped normal system is solved directly; no Krylov solve is
applied to the projected operator.

\subsection{Training Protocol}

The bank and the head are trained in two stages, both as auto-decoders:
the coordinate network $g_\phi$ is fitted to the training states through
the vanishing factor of \eqref{eq:bank}, then frozen, and the head
$\head$ is fitted together with a code library $Z$ on the bank-projected
training states. The correction directions $\corr$ are then the principal
components of the head's residual on the training codes, and the
quadrature rules are fitted on reachable states of the dense solver's own
trajectories. Per-cell sizes, cohorts and the offline cost of each stage
are in Appendix~\ref{app:training-schedule}; all training runs on a
single NVIDIA A100 GPU, and every headline uses one checkpoint per PDE,
named by hash in Table~\ref{tab:provenance}.

\section{Experimental Setup and Benchmark Problems}
\label{sec:setup}

All full-order comparators and reduced solves are benchmarked in one
Slurm allocation on one GPU per job to provide a direct
hardware-to-hardware comparison; \emph{no ratio is ever formed across
jobs or GPUs}. Table~\ref{tab:problems} specifies each cell and
Table~\ref{tab:spec} the sampled families. Burgers 2D at $256^2$ with one
frozen checkpoint is the hero; Poisson, heat and the reflective wave are
the linear cases; the L-shaped Poisson domain is the cell where no fast
transform exists.

\paragraph{Problems.}
\emph{Burgers (hyperbolic):} $u_t+u(u_x+u_y)=\nu\Delta u$ on $(0,1)^2$,
on $256^2$ intervals with a frozen-checkpoint ladder from $64^2$ to
$1024^2$; errors are against the same job's converged full-order solve
on the same grid ($\nPanelRefDiscretisation\,\%$ discretisation error at
$256^2$ against a $4096^2$ reference). \emph{Poisson (elliptic):}
$-\Delta u = f$ with sources from the family in Table~\ref{tab:spec};
ground truth is the verified discrete solution from the direct sine
transform, not an analytic field; evaluated at $256^2$ and $1024^2$ on
the square and on the L-shaped domain $(0,1)^2\setminus[\tfrac12,1)^2$
at $64^2$ to $512^2$, where a sparse direct solve replaces the transform.
\emph{Heat and waves:} $\partial_t u - \kappa \Delta u = 0$ with
Crank--Nicolson and $u_{tt}=c^2\Delta u$ with reflective walls, with the
exact modal propagation of the bank as the $q=R$ rung.

\paragraph{Metrics and timing.}
We report the relative $L^2$ error against the reference of each cell,
maximised over cases and output times; for Burgers two maxima are
reported everywhere and neither is chosen, worst over \emph{all} times
(bounded below by the decoder's compression of the supplied field at
$t=0$) and worst over the \emph{evolved} times $t>0$. Every cost is the
median over repetitions of a completed device computation, timed after a
burn-in with device synchronisation, arms interleaved in a recorded
random order, in float64; offline setup is charged separately.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
FNO, a PDEBench-style U-Net and a Transolver are trained on the
identical dataset and split with recorded index hashes, an equal wall
budget and the same validation-selection rule; the FNO is timed in the
same allocation as the ROM, the U-Net and Transolver are not, so no cost
statement is made for them. The linear POD-LSPG baseline runs through the same weak objective,
tests, solver and stopping rule. Full-order comparators are always in the
same job: preconditioned Newton at several tolerances (Burgers); the
direct sine transform and tuned conjugate gradients (Poisson, heat,
waves); sparse direct and IC(0)-PCG (L-shape). The shallow-masked-autoencoder NM-ROM of \citet{KimChoi2022NMROM} is not
run under this protocol and is not compared. Every table names its job
id, GPU and checkpoint (Table~\ref{tab:provenance}), every solve carries
its exit reason, and every number is emitted by one generator from
audited records.

\section{Numerical Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate NM-ROM against a Fourier Neural Operator, a U-Net and a
Transolver trained on the same data, against POD-LSPG at several ranks,
and against tuned full-order solvers in the same job. Every number below
is a single training seed on opened development cases, one checkpoint
per PDE, except where three seeds are stated; costs are compared only
inside one allocation. Table~\ref{tab:tunability} (appendix) and
Figure~\ref{fig:family} report the headline family at $256^2$. The losses
come first.

\subsection{Comparison against Neural Operators and Full-Order Solvers}
\label{sec:results:operators}\label{sec:exp:panel}

Two plain sentences first. At $256^2$ a well-tuned full-order solver and
the FNO are both cheaper and more accurate than every reduced model, ours
included. At $1024^2$ the three cheapest quadrature rungs are on the
non-dominated set, on the evolved-times metric only, and the cheapest
reduced query falls from $\nPanelCheapestRatio\times$ to
$\nPanelTenTwentyFourCheapestRatio\times$ the cost of the cheapest
same-job full-order setting; we do not call that a crossover until the
$512^2$ panel now queued brackets it.

\paragraph{Nothing reduced is on the frontier at $256^2$.}
Table~\ref{tab:tunability} and Figure~\ref{fig:family} come from one
allocation on one A100 (job \provPanelJob, \nPanelSubjectCount{} timed
subjects: correction rungs, POD-LSPG at ranks \nPanelPodRanks, the
unrestricted bank, the FNO, and full-order Newton at several settings).
On (GPU ms, worst \evolved\ error)
\textbf{\nPanelReducedNonDomEvolved{} of the \nPanelReducedCount{}
admissible reduced subjects are non-dominated} once the full-order
controls are in, and likewise on the \alltimes\ metric. The most accurate
reduced subject is POD-512 at $\nPanelPodFiveTwelveErr\,\%$ and
$\nPanelPodFiveTwelveMs$\,ms; \nPanelFomBeatingMostAccurate{} same-job
full-order settings are cheaper and at least as accurate (the cheapest
at $\nPanelFomNtThreeFineErr\,\%$ and $\nPanelFomNtThreeFineMs$\,ms), and
the FNO reaches $\nPanelFnoErr\,\%$ at $\nPanelFnoMs$\,ms. On the
\evolved\ metric POD-512 beats the best dense rung on both axes; on the
\alltimes\ metric the unrestricted bank does. Both are printed because
the metric decides.

\paragraph{At $1024^2$ the statement is mesh-qualified.}
In a second allocation on one H200 (job \provPanelTenTwentyFourJob;
Table~\ref{tab:tunability-tentwentyfour}; only same-job ratios are
compared with $256^2$), \nPanelTenTwentyFourReducedNonDomEvolved{} of the
\nPanelTenTwentyFourReducedCount{} admissible reduced subjects are
non-dominated on (GPU ms, worst \evolved\ error): the $q=0$, $16$ and
$32$ rungs with rules transferred from $256^2$. On the \alltimes\ metric
no reduced subject is non-dominated at either mesh, because the $t=0$
compression
($\nPanelTenTwentyFourFrontierTzeroMin$--$\nPanelTenTwentyFourFrontierTzeroMax\,\%$
on those rungs) bounds it: which metric is the headline decides whether
this paragraph reports a gain. The transferred rules at $q\ge64$ miss the
primary bar at $1024^2$; why is open.

\paragraph{Neural operators on the same data.}
Neural operators trained on the same data are more accurate than the
reduced model, and the one that was timed against it is also cheaper
(Table~\ref{tab:operators}). Worst error on the matched eight-case cohort:
U-Net-small $\nOpEightUnetSmall$, Transolver-refine
$\nOpEightTsolRefine$, \textbf{ROM $\nOpEightRom$}, FNO-large
$\nOpEightFnoLarge$, efficient full-order solver
$\nOpEightFomEfficient\,\%$: $\nOpArmsBeatingRom$ operator arms beat the
ROM and every FNO capacity is worse than it. An
earlier FNO-only finding that the ROM is the more accurate model was an
artefact of the family and is withdrawn; the comparison survives a
float64 and a second-seed control (Appendix~\ref{app:extended:operators}),
every operator number is a lower bound, and on Poisson the gap is larger
(Table~\ref{tab:operators-poisson}). The operators' own knob, evaluation resolution, is usable for
\nResUsableList{} only (Table~\ref{tab:resolution}); ``one accuracy--cost
point per trained operator'' is withdrawn, but $q$ moves what the model
can represent, which a coarser grid does not.

\subsection{Best Configurations per Problem and Resolution}
\label{sec:results:best}

The same trained decoder serves every resolution. With one frozen
checkpoint per PDE transferred across $64$--$1024$ intervals, the cached
reduced solve costs $\nMeshBurgersCachedFirst\to\nMeshBurgersCachedLast$\,ms
on Burgers while the unknowns grow $\nMeshBurgersUnknownGrowth\times$
(Table~\ref{tab:mesh}); the complete query grows
$\nMeshBurgersCompleteRatio\times$ through dense input and output. There
is no crossover against the cheapest same-job full-order arm at any rung
on the square (FOM/ROM \nMeshBurgersFomOverRomRange{} on Burgers,
\nMeshPoissonFomOverRomRange{} on Poisson), and a kernel port at
bit-level parity buys $\nSpeedGpuTwoFiftySix$ at $256^2$
(Table~\ref{tab:speed}). Moving between operating points never requires
retraining.

\subsection{Which Knob to Turn}
\label{sec:results:tunability}

The rank $q$ is the one deployment-time knob that moves accuracy; the
quadrature rule and the tolerance move cost; the head's training data
and capacity move the floor and need retraining (Tables~\ref{tab:qxm},
\ref{tab:knobs}, \ref{tab:training}).

\paragraph{Rank against test count: the family at fixed $M$.}
The rank alone moves the error: hold the test count fixed, add correction
directions, and the error falls at every step while the cost rises. The
crossed grid (Table~\ref{tab:qxm}) runs $q\in\{0,\dots,256\}$ against
fixed $M\in\{256,1088\}$ and the schedules $M\in\{4,8,16\}(K+q)$ in
\nQxmJobCount{} jobs whose shared cells agree to $\nQxmAnchorWorstAll$
relative. At $M=256$ the ladder spans only $\nQxmTwoFiftySixErrSpan\times$
in error and \textbf{fails} the pre-registered bar (monotone, at least
three non-dominated points, at least $2\times$ on both axes, nothing
early-stopped). \textbf{At $M=\nQxmFixedM$ the ladder $q=0,64,128,256$,
inside one job, is monotone, every rung converged, and spans
$\nQxmErrSpan\times$ in evolved error for $\nQxmCostSpan\times$ in cost
with $\nQxmNonDom$ non-dominated points}, and passes. $M=1088$ was chosen
as the headline after the grid ran, by the lane's rule (the largest fixed
$M$ holding every rung to $q=256$), not pre-registered; the $q=\nQxmExtQtop$
extension did not converge and enters no span, and raising $M$ at $q=256$
saturates at $M^\star=\nQxmMstarTwoFiftySix$ with diminishing return past
it. Repeated on \nSeedsCount{} training seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:seeds}), the scheduled ladder is monotone on
\nSeedsMonotoneEvolved{} of \nSeedsCount{} and meets the knob bar on
\nSeedsKnobBar{} of \nSeedsCount{}; the sealed cohort is not yet opened.

\paragraph{Quadrature and tolerance move cost.}
Quadrature is a cost lever at equal error ($\nPanelEqSpeedupZero\times$
at $q=0$, $\nPanelEqSpeedupOneTwoEight\times$ at $q=128$,
four-decimal-identical error) and the tolerance $10^{-6}\to10^{-3}$
removes a further $\nPanelTolSavingZero$--$\nPanelTolSavingOneTwoEight\,\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:tunability}). Of the two rule sets run side by side, the
one the earlier ladder used breaks upward at $q=256$, where its rule
passes only the secondary bar; the replication-selected set is monotone
at both tolerances,
$\nPanelEqtopTwinSpeedupMin$--$\nPanelEqtopTwinSpeedupMax\times$ cheaper
than its dense twins, its top two rules single-draw and never called
certified. The solver knobs alone (Table~\ref{tab:knobs}) move cost far
more than accuracy, which is why $q$ is the primary knob.

\paragraph{Validate on reachable states, then re-draw.}
\label{sec:exp:eq}
A quadrature rule has to be judged on the states the solver reaches, not
on how well it fits the states it was built from; and that judgement
itself changes from one draw of the rule to the next. A rule's NNLS fit
residual predicts nothing about its error on the states the solver visits
(Figure~\ref{fig:cert}; a static-snapshot rule fitting to
$\nEqtopStaticWorstFit$ reaches $\rho_{\max}=\nEqtopStaticWorstRho$
held-out, four times the primary bar).
\textbf{Then the pre-registered replication re-drew every construction} (Table~\ref{tab:replication}): $\rho_{\max}$
moves by $\nEqtopSpreadMin$--$\nEqtopSpreadMax\times$ within one
construction, and the ladder's rules are \textbf{confirmed at
$q=\nEqtopConfirmedRungs$ and marginal at $q=\nEqtopMarginalRungs$}. The
ladder's errors therefore stand only as measured with the
SHA256-identified rules that happened to pass; a single passing rule is
not a certificate of its construction --- a re-draw is
(Appendix~\ref{app:extended:eq}).

\subsection{The Head against Linear Maps at Matched Dimension}
\label{sec:results:head}

At the same latent dimension the neural head is more accurate than any
linear map in the same bank; per millisecond it is not the best choice.
At matched $k=16$ in one frozen bank per PDE, the head is compared with
the optimal rank-$k$ affine map, a quadratic map, the unrestricted bank,
and POD-LSPG at ranks $8$--$128$ through the same solver
(Tables~\ref{tab:head-burgers}, \ref{tab:head-poisson}). \textbf{The
matched-dimension result is the claim}: on Burgers the head reaches
$\nAblBurgersNeural\,\%$ worst same-grid error against
$\nAblBurgersLinear\,\%$ for the best linear map and
$\nAblBurgersPodSixteen\,\%$ for POD-16; larger POD ranks depend on the
metric (Appendix~\ref{app:extended:head}). On
Poisson at $1024^2$ the head wins per dimension ($\nAblPoissonNeural\,\%$
against $\nAblPoissonLinear\,\%$ linear) and loses per millisecond
(POD-128: $\nAblPoissonPodOneTwentyEight\,\%$ at
$\nAblPoissonPodOneTwentyEightMs$\,ms against the head's
$\nAblPoissonNeuralMs$\,ms), because that query is dominated by projection
and decode. In three layers (Table~\ref{tab:layers}) the reduction layer
sits $\nAblBurgersReductionOverFloor\times$ above the floor and the solver
layer costs $\nAblBurgersSolverGapPp$\,pp: the head is the binding layer
(Table~\ref{tab:training}).

\subsection{Where the Family Collapses: Linear PDEs, Navier--Stokes, and the L-shaped Domain}
\label{sec:results:collapse}

On the linear PDEs there is no trade to make: the corrections are solved
exactly, and the top rung of the ladder is both the most accurate point
and the cheapest. Poisson at $1024^2$ (Table~\ref{tab:linear}): the rungs
$q=0,\dots,256$ run
$\nPlinTenTwentyFourQzeroErr\to\nPlinTenTwentyFourQtwoFiftySixErr\,\%$ at
a flat cost, and the top rung $q=R$ lands on the floor at
$\nPlinTenTwentyFourTopErr\,\%$ as the \emph{cheapest} point
($\nPlinTenTwentyFourTopMs$\,ms; the direct transform is exact at
$\nPlinTenTwentyFourDstMs$\,ms). Heat (Table~\ref{tab:heat}) and the
reflective wave (Table~\ref{tab:waves}) behave the same way
(Appendix~\ref{app:extended:linear}). \textbf{The collapse is confounded
with a weak bank, and we say so}: on both linear cells the learned bank is
three to four times worse than a POD basis of the same rank, while on
Burgers the free bank and POD-512 are comparable as banks. No reduced arm
beats the direct solve on any of the three.

\textbf{Navier--Stokes collapses for a different reason.} On 2D
incompressible flow (a nonlinear residual) the family was gated before
any ladder ran (Table~\ref{tab:ns}): at both $K=16$ and $K=32$ the head's
held-out oracle beats POD-$K$ by only $\nNsOracleRatio\times$ and
$\nNsKthirtyTwoOracleRatio\times$ where the pre-registered bar was
$\nNsOracleBar$, so no ladder was built; the limit is head
generalisation, and at $t=0$ POD-32 is the more accurate of the two
(POD/oracle $\nNsKthirtyTwoPodOverOracleTzero$), a clean case of linear
beating nonlinear where the data lie in a low-dimensional linear
subspace. So the correction-rank trade needs \emph{both} a residual
nonlinear in the coefficients and a manifold that beats POD held-out;
Poisson, heat and waves lack the first, this cell the second, and Burgers
on the square had both yet still lost on cost.

\textbf{On the L-shaped domain the trade does exist, and it widens with
the mesh.} In plain terms: the reduced solve becomes the cheaper option
between $128^2$ and $256^2$, and by $512^2$ it is
$\nLshapeNeuralCheaperFiveTwelve\times$ cheaper than the direct solve at
about two percent error. No fast transform applies there, so the
full-order comparator is a sparse direct solve whose cost climbs with the
mesh ($\nLshapeSpluMsSixtyFour$ to $\nLshapeSpluMsFiveTwelve$\,ms from
$64^2$ to $512^2$) while the head at $q=64$ stays nearly flat
(\nLshapeNeuralMsTrend\,ms). Table~\ref{tab:lshape-solve} gives the
non-dominated set per mesh, one job per mesh, every ratio inside its job:
at $256^2$ the head at $q=64$ reaches $\nLshapeNeuralErr\,\%$ at
$\nLshapeNeuralMs$\,ms against $\nLshapeSpluMsTwoFiftySix$\,ms,
$\nLshapeNeuralCheaper\times$ cheaper; at $512^2$ it reaches
$\nLshapeNeuralErrFiveTwelve\,\%$ at $\nLshapeNeuralMsFiveTwelve$\,ms
against $\nLshapeSpluMsFiveTwelve$\,ms,
$\nLshapeNeuralCheaperFiveTwelve\times$ cheaper, and is the most accurate
reduced subject on the set. The win is on cost alone: no reduced arm
beats the direct solve on accuracy at any mesh, nothing is claimed beyond
$512^2$, and every L-shape error quoted is the optimistic end of a range
($\nLshapeDevelopmentWorst\,\%$ worst best-found on development sources
against $\nLshapeValidationWorst\,\%$ on validation sources,
Table~\ref{tab:lshape}).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\label{sec:limitations}
(i) Every headline rests on one training seed and one checkpoint per PDE;
the Burgers scheduled ladder was repeated on \nSeedsCount{} seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:seeds}; \nSeedsUnconvergedCells{} did not converge), and
its sealed cohort is not yet opened. (ii)
Discretisations are finite-difference on uniform Cartesian grids. (iii)
Benchmarks are restricted to 2D; the Navier--Stokes reduced model failed
its pre-registered gate at $K=16$ and $K=32$
(\S\ref{sec:results:collapse}), and the development cohorts are small
(six opened Burgers cases, eight wave and twelve Poisson). (iv) No cold-start comparison against a shallow
masked-autoencoder ROM; the linear skip is not ablated. (v) Operator
numbers are lower bounds (\nOpLaneImproving{} U-Net/Transolver arms and
\nOpFnoImproving{} FNO arms were still improving at their budget). (vi)
Quadrature rules above $q=32$ are marginal; the headline ladder's $M$ was
chosen after the fact; cross-job spread of an identical cell is
$\nQxmAnchorSpreadPct\,\%$; the $1024^2$ frontier statement rests on one job and the evolved metric
(a $512^2$ panel is queued). (vii) No theory: no convergence guarantee and
no quadrature-error bound on unseen states.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We built an NM-ROM for elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic PDEs whose
distinguishing property is a deployment-time accuracy/cost family traced
by a single trained model: at inference, the correction rank $q$ and
three solver-side knobs --- the iteration cap, the stopping tolerance,
and the Empirical Quadrature sample count --- trade accuracy for cost,
spanning $\nQxmErrSpan\times$ in error for $\nQxmCostSpan\times$ in cost on
2D Burgers in one allocation. This is the lever a neural operator does not expose from one trained
model: its evaluation grid moves cost, not what the model can represent
(\S\ref{sec:results:operators}). The framework does not beat a tuned full-order solver or
a neural operator trained on the same data at $256^2$ on the square, and
we report that; its wins are cost-only, on the L-shaped domain where no
fast transform applies and, on the evolved-times metric, at $1024^2$.
Mechanically, the decoder is a learned non-linear manifold inside a
linear bank, the PDE is enforced by least-squares Petrov--Galerkin
projection onto fixed weak tests, and Empirical Quadrature decouples the
one nonlinear term from mesh size. The trade is real where the residual is nonlinear in the coefficients and no
fast transform makes the full-order solve cheap, and it collapses where
neither holds. Natural next steps are a harder Burgers regime at lower viscosity, a
Navier--Stokes head that beats POD held-out, and unstructured meshes.
