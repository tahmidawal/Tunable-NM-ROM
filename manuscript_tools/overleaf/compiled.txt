009                                                A BSTRACT
010
011            A trained neural operator gives one accuracy–cost trade-off per model, and chang-
012            ing it means retraining.1 We present a nonlinear-manifold reduced-order model
013            (NM-ROM) whose balance between accuracy and speed is chosen when the model
014            is used. Its spatial bank, a set of learned functions of space, is ordered once by
015            importance after training, so a query chooses how many bank functions to use
016            and whether to solve with a small nonlinear head or in the linear span of the bank,
017            without changing a learned weight. Every linear term is precomputed exactly,
018
               the discrete PDE residual is solved by least-squares Petrov–Galerkin projection
               onto fixed tests, and the nonlinear term is evaluated on a sparse set of nodes. We
019
               compare every setting with the fastest tested solver that reaches the same accuracy;
020
               on the new problems that solver may choose its mesh, integrator and step. On a
021            three-dimensional Navier–Stokes family with sharper interacting vortices and lower
022            viscosity, on 128 fresh test cases and three independently trained models, the head
023            reaches 0.28–0.42 % worst-case error at bank width 192, and a faster setting 0.35–
024            0.48 % at 5.1–10.2× the speed of the matched solver from a 450-configuration
025            ladder; the linear span of the same bank is unstable (7.7–70 %). On unsteady
026            flow past a cylinder, one trained bank meets six acceptance targets at a held-out
027            Reynolds number at widths 16 to 32, with a rollout 25–54× faster than the cheapest
028            finite-element run that meets the same targets. The advantage appears where the
029
               matched solver needs a fine mesh and the solution family is low-dimensional; on
               constant-coefficient box problems transform solvers are mostly faster, and we use
030
               those problems as correctness checks.

033   1    I NTRODUCTION
034
035   Two lines of work aim to make partial differential equation (PDE) solutions fast to compute: neural
036   operators and reduced-order models (ROMs), and both have advanced quickly. Neural operators
037   (Li et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2021; Kovachki et al., 2023) now include attention-based models (Wu
038   et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2025) and pretrained foundation models (Herde et al., 2024; Hao et al., 2024;
039   Holzschuh et al., 2025), and are fast to evaluate once trained (Azizzadenesheli et al., 2024). In
040   parallel, nonlinear-manifold ROMs (NM-ROMs) have moved from dense autoencoders (Lee and
041   Carlberg, 2020; Y. Kim et al., 2022) to domain-decomposed (Diaz et al., 2024), neural-field (Chen
042
      et al., 2023; M. Kim et al., 2024; Berman and Peherstorfer, 2024) and hyper-reduced Navier–Stokes
      models (Romor et al., 2025), while keeping the projection of the governing equations used by linear
043
      projection-based ROMs (Benner et al., 2015; Carlberg et al., 2011). Hesthaven et al. (2026) survey
044
      these nonlinear methods for transport-dominated problems.
045
046   Two properties decide whether such a model can stand in for a full-order solver: whether accuracy
047   can be traded for speed when the model is used, and whether it is faster than a well-chosen solver of
048
      the same accuracy. Full-order methods (FOMs) have the first property, since their mesh, tolerance and
      time step are set per query (each new problem instance to solve). A trained neural operator gives one
049
      trade-off between accuracy and wall-clock time at a given mesh, and changing it in general requires
050
      retraining (de Hoop et al., 2022); comparisons against weak numerical baselines can also overstate
051   their speed (McGreivy and Hakim, 2024). Linear ROMs can require many basis functions for moving

059

060
      correctness checks, on Poisson and heat problems. Our NM-ROM builds the solution from a spatial
061   bank, a set of R functions of space, each a small neural network of the coordinates, whose weighted
062   sum gives the solution; the weights come from a small nonlinear head. After training, the bank is
063   ordered once by how much of the training data each of its functions carries. A query then chooses
064   how many of the ordered functions to use, R′ , and whether to solve with the nonlinear head or in the
065   linear span of those functions, without changing a learned weight. Because each bank function is a
066   small network of the coordinates, for Poisson, heat and Burgers the learned functions are shared by
067   every mesh (the Navier–Stokes results of Table 1 use per-mesh POD banks); the reduced operators are
068   assembled once per mesh, and we charge that assembly as an offline cost. On periodic boxes a POD
069
      basis interpolated across meshes transfers as well as the coordinate network, so there the network
      provides a single mesh-free object rather than higher accuracy. Our intended use is repeated queries
070
      within a parameterised family after offline learning and assembly. The learned network provides a
071
      compact representation; each query still determines its coefficients from the PDE residual, without
072   access to the reference solution.

      3     M ETHODOLOGY
163
164
      A full-order solve has one unknown per grid node, so its cost grows with the mesh size N . For each
165   query we instead solve the same discretised PDE in a few unknowns, at a cost that barely depends on
166   N apart from forming the output field. This needs three ingredients. First, a compact set of candidate
167   solutions, the trial manifold (§3.1): the solution is a weighted sum of the functions in the spatial
168   bank, and its weights are either produced by the nonlinear head from a few latent unknowns or solved
169   for directly on the first R′ bank functions. Second, a way to choose the right candidate without
170   knowing the answer (§3.2). Third, a way to evaluate the PDE without visiting all N nodes (§3.3):
171   linear terms are computed once offline, and the nonlinear term is sampled at a few nodes (in three
172   dimensions, it is evaluated with a precomputed quadratic tensor). Figure 1 shows the whole pipeline.
173
      Throughout, u ∈ RN is the full-order discrete state on a uniform grid and K ∈ RN ×N is the sparse
      finite-difference matrix of −∆.

      than the training range the median error is 5.7 and 16.6 % on two exploratory development regimes;

506
      harder family and one horizon (T = 0.2) at moderate Reynolds numbers; we make no claim about
507

515   6     C ONCLUSION AND F UTURE W ORK
516
517   One trained NM-ROM gives a ladder of accuracy–cost settings at query time, and its head makes a
518   small model accurate where the linear span of the same bank is not. Against the fastest solver of equal
519   accuracy it is faster where that solver needs a fine mesh and the solution family is low-dimensional,
520   and not where a coarse mesh or a fast transform suffices. Next are wider parameter bands, longer
521   horizons and the least-squares Petrov–Galerkin solve on general geometry.

877   A.7    S OLVER CONSTANTS
878
      With rw the scaled tested residual and J its Jacobian, the stopping test is scale free, ∥J ⊤ rw ∥2 ≤
879
      ηtol ∥J∥F ∥rw ∥2 , so one tolerance serves every mesh and width. The Poisson, L-shape and heat
880
      settings of Table 3 are span solves with no stopping test. Two-dimensional Burgers uses ηtol = 10−2
881   at 2562 –10242 and 10−3 at 20482 and 40962 , with at most 600 attempts per step. Three-dimensional
882   Burgers solves the first three steps to ηtol = 10−3 (at most 50 attempts) and then takes one sweep per
883   step. Navier–Stokes takes a fixed three Gauss–Newton sweeps per step with fixed damping λ = 10−6 .
884   Damping starts at λ0 = 10−6 (Poisson, Burgers in two and three dimensions) or 10−4 (heat), is
885   carried between time steps, is divided by 3 on acceptance (down to 10−12 ) and multiplied by 10 on
886   rejection (up to 1014 ). The bank floor is the projection error onto the span of the bank, which no
887   solve can beat.

1570
1571   Problem. DFG 2D-2 channel and cylinder (Schäfer and Turek, 1996), mean inflow 1, kinematic
1572   viscosity 0.1/Re, gradient-form outlet. Trajectories start from the steady state at Reynolds 20 with a
1573   ramped inflow and a one-off asymmetric kick; each records a window of 1.7 s (about five shedding
1574   periods), saved every 1/400 s. Training Reynolds numbers 90, 100, 110; development 95; held-out
1575   105, opened once. The training data come from a curved P4/P3 Taylor–Hood reference with BDF2;
1576
       the model lives on the P2/P1 export mesh with 96,781 unknowns.
1577   Model. The bank represents the velocity minus a lift (the mean training velocity), which carries the
1578   inflow. Its boundary factor is a product of smooth distance factors to the inlet, the channel walls
1579   and the cylinder surface. The bank is trained on the transient training states and ordered as in §3.1;
1580   widths 16 and 24 are prefixes of the width-32 bank. Each velocity width r is paired with r pressure
1581   functions and one supremizer per pressure function, and the rollout is a fully implicit BDF2 Galerkin
1582
       projection with all reduced operators assembled once. A POD basis of equal width, rolled out the
       same way, performs the same within 1 %.

1586   error ≤ 2×10−2 , all fixed in advance.
